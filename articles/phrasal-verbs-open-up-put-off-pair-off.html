Introduction: Phrasal Verbs as Social Instruments

Phrasal verbs sit at the informal, idiomatic heart of English. Among them, a small group governs social and interpersonal behaviour: how we confide, how we defer or discourage, and how we form couples. Open up, put off, and pair off each carry distinct social meanings — and each hides more senses than a first glance suggests.

1. Open Up

[ The Senses of "Open Up" ]

   Literal ──► open a container / business ("open up the shop")
   Communicative ──► confide ("open up about her past")
   Expansive ──► make accessible ("open up new markets")
   Spatial ──► become visible / widen ("the valley opened up")
   Aggressive ──► begin firing / attacking ("the troops opened up")

A. Literal: To Open Something

To open a container, a building, or a business; often with the sense of starting the day’s operation: * “The barista opened up the café at six.” * “We opened up the crate and found the painting intact.”

B. Communicative: To Confide (the key modern sense)

To become more open, communicative, and emotionally forthcoming — an intransitive use, usually with about or to: * “He finally opened up about his difficult childhood.” * “She opened up to her therapist more than she had to anyone.” * “A good manager creates the trust that lets people open up.”

C. Expansive: To Make Accessible

To create new possibilities, markets, or access: * “The internet opened up opportunities for remote workers worldwide.” * “The discovery opened up an entirely new field of research.”

D. Spatial: To Become Visible or Wider

  • “After the pass, the valley opened up before us.”

E. Aggressive (informal): To Begin Firing or Attacking

  • “The artillery opened up at dawn.”

Syntax

Pattern Example
open up (intr.) “She finally opened up.”
open up to + person “He opened up to me.”
open up about + topic “They opened up about the accident.”
open sth up “The policy opened the market up.”

Antonym: clam up (refuse to speak), shut down (emotionally close off), bottle up (suppress feelings).

Model Sentence: “It took months of quiet patience before the witness opened up about what she had seen.”

2. Put Off

Put off is covered together with the wider put family in the companion article on the phrasal verbs of put; here we focus on its two core social senses.

[ The Senses of "Put Off" ]

   Temporal ──► postpone ("put off the meeting")
   Deterrent ──► discourage / repel ("the smell put me off")
   Distractive ──► disturb concentration ("don't put me off")

A. To Postpone

To delay an event, decision, or task to a later time: * “They put off the wedding until spring.” * “Stop putting off the dentist.” * “The vote was put off indefinitely.”

Register Verb
Neutral / formal postpone, defer, delay, adjourn
Informal put off, push back, kick the can down the road

B. To Deter, Discourage, or Repel

To make someone lose interest, enthusiasm, or courage — very often about a thing or activity: * “The high price put me off buying it.” * “His rudeness put her off the whole idea.” * “Don’t let one bad experience put you off.” * be put off by: “We were put off by the smell.”

C. To Distract

To interrupt someone’s concentration or flow: * “Don’t put me off while I’m aiming.” * “The noise put him off his stroke.”

Syntax

Pattern Example
put off + noun/gerund “put off the decision,” “put off deciding”
put off + object + until “put it off until Friday”
put sb off (sth) “The crowd put him off his serve.”
be put off by “I was put off by the attitude.”

Nuance: Postpone is neutral and cannot mean “deter”; put off covers both. “The weather postponed the match” is fine, but “the weather put off the match” is not idiomatic — you put off a person (deter), or put off an event (postpone).

Model Sentence: “The interviewer’s sneering tone put off several talented candidates — and the board, sensing the damage, put off the final hiring decision.”

3. Pair Off

[ The Senses of "Pair Off" ]

   Distributive ──► divide a group into pairs ("pair off the students")
   Coupling ──► form romantic/social couples ("the guests paired off")
   Political (BrE) ──► MPs of opposing sides agree to abstain ("pairing")

A. Distributive: To Divide into Pairs

To arrange people or things into twos, often for an activity or game: * “The teacher paired off the students for the debate.” * “Pair off the socks before you put them away.”

B. Coupling: To Form a Couple

To join together, especially romantically or socially: * “By the end of the evening, most of the guests had paired off.” * “The dancers paired off for the waltz.”

C. British Political Idiom: “Pairing”

In the UK Parliament, pairing is an arrangement whereby two MPs from opposing parties agree to abstain simultaneously, so that both can be absent without affecting a vote.

Syntax

Pattern Example
pair off (intr.) “They paired off quickly.”
pair sb off (with sb) “She paired him off with a colleague.”
pair off into + group “The class paired off into fours.”

Nuance: Pair Off vs. Pair Up

Phrasal Verb Nuance
Pair off Divide a group into pairs; often romantic or social; can imply couples.
Pair up Join with someone for a shared task; cooperative and neutral.
Pair with Combine two things (e.g., “pair wine with cheese”).

“The instructor paired us up for the exercise” (task) vs. “the guests paired off by midnight” (romance).

Model Sentence: “For the relay, the coach paired off the runners by speed, and by the second lap they had paired up into seamless teams.”

4. Master Comparison Matrix

Phrasal Verb Core Senses Register Key Pattern Antonym / Counterpart
Open up Open; confide; make accessible; start firing Neutral/informal open up about / to clam up, shut down
Put off Postpone; deter; distract Informal put off sth / put sb off bring forward; encourage
Pair off Divide into pairs; form couples Neutral/informal pair off (with) split up; separate

5. Common Errors

❌ “He opened up his feelings about the loss.” → ✅ “He opened up about the loss.” (intransitive about) ❌ “The rain put off the match.” → ✅ “The rain postponed the match.” (put off an event is possible but less natural than postpone; put off more naturally means to deter a person) ❌ “The teacher paired up the students into couples.” → ✅ “The teacher paired off the students.” (pair off for couples/division; pair up for tasks)

6. Diagnostic Synthesis

“At the team-building retreat, the facilitator paired off the newcomers for a trust exercise. At first, one engineer kept to himself, but as the afternoon wore on he began to open up about his anxiety in large groups. The boisterous games, however, seemed to put off the quieter participants — so the organizer wisely put off the final activity until after dinner, when everyone had opened up enough to enjoy it.”

7. Knowledge Retention Quiz

1. Which sense of “open up” is intransitive and social? A) To open a crate B) To confide in someone C) To begin firing D) To open a shop

2. What does “put off” mean in “the smell put me off”? A) Postponed me B) Deterred or repelled me C) Distracted me D) Attracted me

3. Which is the best antonym of “open up” in its communicative sense? A) pair off B) put off C) clam up D) open out

4. What is the difference between “pair off” and “pair up”? A) No difference. B) “Pair off” often divides a group into couples; “pair up” joins people for a shared task. C) “Pair off” is formal; “pair up” is archaic. D) “Pair up” is only for socks.

5. Which sentence is correct? A) She opened up her grief. B) She opened up about her grief. C) She opened up on her grief. D) She opened grief up about.

Answer Key & Rationale

  1. B. Open up meaning “confide” is intransitive and takes about/to.
  2. B. Here put off means to deter or repel.
  3. C. Clam up means to refuse to speak, the opposite of confiding.
  4. B. Pair off suggests dividing into couples (often romantic); pair up means joining for a task.
  5. B. The confiding sense takes open up about.

# Introduction: Phrasal Verbs as Social Instruments

Phrasal verbs sit at the informal, idiomatic heart of English. Among them, a small group governs **social and interpersonal behaviour**: how we confide, how we defer or discourage, and how we form couples. *Open up*, *put off*, and *pair off* each carry distinct social meanings — and each hides more senses than a first glance suggests.

## 1. Open Up

```text
[ The Senses of "Open Up" ]

   Literal ──► open a container / business ("open up the shop")
   Communicative ──► confide ("open up about her past")
```

### A. Literal: To Open Something
To open a container, a building, or a business; often with the sense of *starting the day's operation*:
* *"The barista **opened up** the café at six."*

* *"He finally **opened up about** his difficult childhood."*

* *"After the pass, the valley **opened up** before us."*

### E. Aggressive (informal): To Begin Firing or Attacking
* *"The artillery **opened up** at dawn."*

### Syntax
| Pattern | Example |
| :--- | :--- |
| open up (intr.) | *"She finally opened up."* |

| open up about + topic | *"They opened up about the accident."* |

## 5. Common Errors

> ❌ *"He opened up his feelings about the loss."* → ✅ *"He **opened up about** the loss."* (intransitive *about*)
