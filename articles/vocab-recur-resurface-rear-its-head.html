Introduction: Things That Come Back

Some events happen once. Others return — periodically, unpredictably, or menacingly. English distinguishes these subtly: a problem may recur (happen repeatedly), resurface (come back to view), reemerge (appear again), or rear its head (return, often with menace). This article maps the vocabulary of return with attention to register and usage traps.

[ THE RETURN SPECTRUM ]

  happen again ──► recur / reoccur ──► resurface / reemerge ──► rear its head
  (neutral)        (repeatedly)          (appear again)           (ominous return)

  illness ──► relapse / recrudesce ──► flare-up
  (medical)

1. Recur

Phonetics: /rɪˈkɜːr/.

Etymological Root

From Latin recurrere = re- (“back, again”) + currere (“to run”). Literally, “to run back again.”

Meaning

To occur again, especially repeatedly or periodically: * “The same problem recurs every winter.” * “A recurring theme in her work is exile.” * “The error recurred throughout the dataset.”

Derived Forms

  • Recurring (adjective): happening repeatedly — “a recurring nightmare,” “a recurring charge.”
  • Recurrence (noun): the fact of occurring again — “the recurrence of the disease.”
  • Recurrent (adjective, formal/technical): “recurrent infections.”

Usage note: Recurring is the standard adjective (“recurring dream”). The alternative reoccurring is a common but often criticized variant — see below.

2. Recur vs. Reoccur — A Usage Distinction

Word Meaning Nuance
Recur Happen again, especially repeatedly/periodically Suggests a pattern
Reoccur Happen again (a second time) Suggests a single repetition
  • “The symptoms recur every spring” (a pattern).
  • “The glitch reoccurred after the last update” (a second occurrence).

Guidance: Many careful writers prefer recur for repeated events and reserve reoccur for a one-off second happening. Recurrence is vastly more common than reoccurrence.

3. Resurface

Phonetics: /ˌriːˈsɜːfɪs/.

Three Senses

  1. To come to the surface again (literal): “The submarine resurfaced after hours submerged.”
  2. To appear or become prominent again after being absent, hidden, or forgotten — the key figurative sense: “The scandal resurfaced years later.” / “Old tensions resurfaced at the meeting.”
  3. To put a new surface on a road or path: “The council resurfaced the high street.”

Nuance: Resurface implies something was buried or hidden and has come back into view — originally underwater. It is the natural verb for a returning issue or controversy.

4. Reemerge (Re-emerge)

Phonetics: /ˌriːɪˈmɜːdʒ/.

Meaning: To come out again; to become prominent again after a period of absence: * “The truth reemerged during the trial.” * “A once-extinct species has reemerged in the valley.”

Nuance: Reemerge stresses coming back into view or existence; it pairs naturally with from (“reemerge from hiding”).

Spelling: Both reemerge and re-emerge are acceptable; British styles often prefer the hyphen.

5. Rear Its Head

Meaning

To appear or become evident, especially (of something unpleasant) after a period of quiescence. The intensified form “rear its ugly head” is extremely common: * “Racism has reared its head again in the debate.” * “The problem reared its ugly head during the audit.”

Etymological Note

Rear here is the verb meaning “to raise up, to rise on the hind legs” — the image is a horse or beast lifting its head, or a serpent rising. The phrase conveys that something dangerous or repugnant is emerging into view. The fuller “ugly head” version gained currency in the 20th century.

Nuances

  • Almost always used of something negative (a problem, prejudice, vice, or disease).
  • Suggests a reappearance after suppression, not a first occurrence.
  • Grammatically, the thing is the subject: “The issue reared its head.”

Model Sentence: “Just when the recovery seemed secure, inflation reared its head once more.”

6. Recrudescence — The Formal Technical Term

Phonetics: /ˌriːkruːˈdes.əns/.

Etymological Architecture

From Latin recrudescere = re- (“again”) + crudescere (“to become raw or harsh”), from crudus (“raw, rough”) — the same root as crude. Literally: “to become raw again.”

Meaning

The return of a disease or unpleasant phenomenon after a period of quiescence: * “A recrudescence of the infection after treatment.” * “A recrudescence of violence in the region.”

Register: Formal, chiefly medical and academic. The verb recrudesce exists but is rare.

Nuance: Recrudescence implies a temporary remission followed by return — subtly different from relapse (which also implies deterioration) and from a simple recurrence.

7. Other Words of Return and Revival

Word Meaning Register
Relapse A return to a prior bad condition (illness, addiction) Medical/psych
Resurgence A rising again into prominence or strength Neutral/formal
Revival Restoration to life, vigour, or popularity Neutral
Comeback A return to success or popularity Informal
Renaissance A cultural rebirth Formal/historical
Recrudescence Return of a disease/problem after quiescence Formal/medical
Flare-up A sudden recurrence of a condition or conflict Informal/medical
Rebirth / renascence Spiritual or cultural renewal Literary/formal

Nuance: Resurgence suggests renewed strength (“a resurgence of nationalism”); revival suggests restoring something dormant; comeback is the informal, often entertainment-world choice.

8. Repetition Patterns — The Adjectives

Adjective Meaning Example
Recurring Happening repeatedly “a recurring motif”
Periodic At regular intervals “periodic reviews”
Cyclical Occurring in cycles “cyclical downturns”
Intermittent Stopping and starting “intermittent rain”
Sporadic Occasional, irregular, scattered “sporadic protests”
Episodic Occurring in episodes “episodic memory”
Perennial Lasting or recurring year after year “a perennial favourite”
Chronic Persisting over a long time “chronic pain”

9. Idioms of Return

Idiom Meaning
History repeats itself The same patterns recur across time
Come back to haunt (someone) A past action causes later trouble
Rear its ugly head (Of something bad) to reappear
Back with a vengeance Returning more strongly than before
The same old story A familiar, repeated situation
Everything comes back around Trends/events tend to return
Plus ça change (plus c’est la même chose) The more things change, the more they stay the same
A blast from the past Something unexpectedly recalled or returned from long ago

10. Model Sentences

“The infection had seemed cured, but a recrudescence followed within weeks. Old symptoms recurred, then the original scandal resurfaced; and as if on cue, the allegations reared their ugly head once more, back with a vengeance.”
