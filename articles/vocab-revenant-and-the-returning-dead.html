From Latin venire to the grave and back — how English names those who come again.

Introduction: The One Who Comes Back

English has a great many words for things that return, but only one that names the dead who return in the flesh: revenant. The word is a direct borrowing from French, where it is nothing more exotic than the present participle of the verb revenir, “to come back.” A revenant, etymologically, is simply one who is coming back.

That plain origin is the key to everything interesting about the word. It is not a word about souls — that is ghost, spirit, specter. It is a word about return, and it belongs to a deep and productive Latin family built on venire, “to come.”

[ THE RETURN-ROOT FAMILY ]   Latin: venire = "to come"

  re-  (back)   + venire ──► revenire ──► revenir  ──► REVENANT
                            (to come back)  (French)     (one who returns)
                                                 └──────► revenue   (that which returns)

  ad-  (to)     + venire ──► advenire ──► advent / adventure
  con- (together)+ venire ──► convenire ──► convene / convention
  in-  (into)   + venire ──► invenire  ──► invent / inventory
  inter-(between)+ venire ──► intervenire ──► intervene
  prae- (before)+ venire ──► praevenire ──► prevent
  sub- (under)  + venire ──► subvenire ──► souvenir  (to come to mind)

1. Revenant — Definition and Pronunciation

Pronunciation: /ˈrɛv.ə.nənt/. Stress falls on the first syllable in English (unlike French, where it falls at the end). The final syllable is weak — REV-uh-nunt, not rev-uh-NANT.

Definition (noun): a person who has returned from the dead, especially one who returns in bodily form; more loosely, anyone who returns after a long absence.

Definition (adjective, archaic/literary): returning, coming back — “a revenant spirit.”

  • “The village believed the drowned man had become a revenant.”
  • “He came back into public life like a revenant from a discredited era.”
  • “The novel is haunted by revenants — the unquiet dead and the unwelcome past alike.”

Register: literary, historical, and folklore studies; in contemporary prose it often carries a deliberate archaizing or gothic flavour. It is the precise term where zombie would be anachronistic and ghost would be wrong.

The word’s distinctive claim: a revenant returns bodily. A ghost may be a breath of cold air; a revenant is a corpse that walks back through the door. That physicality is the semantic core.

2. The venire Family: Return, Arrival, and Coming-to-Mind

Because venire (“to come”) is one of the most productive roots in the Latin inheritance, revenant sits inside a large etymological clan. Seeing it whole makes the word easier to fix in memory.

Latin Prefix sense French English
revenire re- “back” revenir revenant, revenue
advenire ad- “to” avenir advent, adventure, avenue
convenire con- “together” convenir convene, convention, covenant
invenire in- “upon” inventer invent, invention, inventory
intervenire inter- “between” intervenir intervene, intervention
praevenire prae- “before” prévenir prevent, prevention
subvenire sub- “up, under” souvenir souvenir, subvention
circumvenire circum- “around” circonvenir circumvent
contravenire contra- “against” contrevenir contravene
supervenire super- “above” survenir supervene
eventus (from evenire) “come out” événement event, eventual

Two Revelations in This Table

  1. Revenue is a cousin of revenant. Revenue descends from the past participle of revenir — literally “that which has returned,” i.e. income coming back to the one who is owed it. The same root that gives us a walking corpse gives us a tax term — a memorable illustration of how far a root can travel.
  2. Souvenir is a cousin too. Subvenire meant “to come up (into the mind),” and French souvenir retains that sense of memory. A souvenir is a memory-object: something that comes back to you.

Going deeper: the ven- forms also surface in venue (a place to which people come), avenue (a way of coming to a place), and provenance/provenience (origin — where a thing comes from). The root runs through English like a road.

3. Revert, Reversion, and Revertant — Cousins by Meaning, Not Root

A word of caution about a word that looks like family:

  • Revert comes from Latin revertere = re- + vertere (“to turn”), not venire (“to come”). It means to turn back — to return to a former state, practice, or owner.
  • Reversion (noun): a return to a previous state; also, in law, the return of property to its original owner or the owner’s heirs (a reversionary interest).
  • Revertant (noun, genetics): an organism or cell that has reverted — a mutant that regains the wild-type phenotype by a second mutation.
Word Root Meaning
Revenant venire “come” One who comes back, especially from the dead
Revert vertere “turn” Turn back to a former state; return to a former owner
Revertant vertere “turn” A mutant that has reverted (genetics)
Reversion vertere “turn” The act of returning; a legal right of return

Usage trap — “revert back”: because revert already contains “back” in its re-, the phrase revert back is a redundancy that careful writers avoid. “The system reverted to its default” — not “reverted back to its default.” The same logic condemns return back, recur back, and reiterate again.

4. The Lexicon of the Undead and the Unquiet

Revenant is a member of a crowded semantic field. English inherited words for the returning dead from at least five traditions — Germanic, Latin, Celtic, Slavic, and West African — and the resulting vocabulary preserves their distinct theologies.

A. The Disembodied Dead (Soul, Not Body)

Word Etymology Core sense
Ghost Old English gāst (breath, spirit) The apparition of a dead person; the soul after death
Spirit Latin spiritus (breath) The non-physical part of a person; also a ghost, and also vitality or mood
Specter / spectre Latin spectrum (appearance, image) A ghost, but especially one that frightens; figuratively, a threatening prospect (“the specter of famine”)
Phantom Greek phantasma (appearance) An apparition, real or imagined; frequently figurative (phantom limb, phantom load)
Apparition Latin apparere (to appear) A striking or unexpected appearance; the neutral, clinical term for a ghostly sighting
Wraith Scots, of obscure origin A ghost; in Scottish lore, sometimes the apparition of a living person, portending death
Shade Old English sceadu (shadow) The classical term for a ghost — the shadows of the dead in Hades; now literary (“the shades of the departed”)
Fetch Old English fæcce A double or apparition of a living person, often a death omen (Irish and English folklore)

B. The Bodily Dead (Corpse, Not Soul)

Word Etymology Core sense
Revenant French revenir A corpse that returns and walks; the neutral, literary term
Zombie Haitian Creole, from West African nzambi (god, spirit) A reanimated corpse without will, in Vodou belief; in cinema, a ravenous horde member
Ghoul Arabic ghūl A demon that robs graves and feeds on corpses; later, a person morbidly fascinated by death
Lich Old English līc (body, corpse) Fantasy: an undead sorcerer whose soul is preserved outside the body (in a phylactery)
Draugr Old Norse draugr The Icelandic undead: a corpse that guards its grave-mound, grows in size, and must be physically destroyed
Wight Old English wiht (creature, being) Archaic “person”; in fantasy, the barrow-wight — a grave-haunting undead
Vampire Slavonic (cf. Serbian vampir) A revenant that leaves the grave to feed on the living; arguably Europe’s most famous revenant type
Mummy Persian/Arabic mūmiyā (bitumen) An embalmed body; in fiction, a deliberate, vengeful revenant
Reanimant Latin re- + anima (soul) Rare coinage for “one reanimated” — useful when you want the concept without the folklore

C. The Haunting Presences (Neither Simply Ghost nor Corpse)

Word Etymology Core sense
Poltergeist German poltern (to make noise) + Geist (ghost) A noisy spirit that moves objects; the German borrowing is literal
Banshee Irish bean sídhe (woman of the fairy mound) A female spirit whose wail announces a death; not an apparition so much as an omen
Doppelgänger German (double + goer) An apparition of a living person; a double
Lemure Latin lemures (restless ghosts) In Roman belief, the unquiet dead of the household; the animal lemur is named after them
Wendigo Algonquian A cannibalistic spirit of the northern forests; not a revenant, but a hunger-spirit
Ghost light / corpse candle — Folkloric lights that precede or follow a death

Etymological footnote: ghoul, poltergeist, and doppelgänger are reminders that the undead lexicon is one of English’s most borrowed semantic fields. Where Germanic and Celtic terms mostly name the appearance (ghost, shade, fetch), Semitic, German, and Norse terms name the behaviour (grave-robbing, noise-making, walking).

5. Revenant vs. Zombie vs. Ghost: The Three-Way Distinction

This is the distinction most worth internalising, because careless writing blurs it.

Revenant Zombie Ghost
What returns The body The body The soul
Will Retains personal intent (often vengeful) Usually none; driven by hunger or a controller Retains personality and memory
Origin European folklore: unquiet dead, excommunicated, oath-breakers Haitian Vodou, then Hollywood Universal
Register Literary, historical, gothic Popular, cinematic, figurative Everyday, neutral
Figurative use “a revenant of the past” — a person or idea returning after long absence “zombie company,” “zombie idea” — something dead that keeps operating “ghost of a chance,” “ghosting someone”
  • Revenant is the word for bodily return with purpose.
  • Zombie is the word for bodily return without will.
  • Ghost is the word for return without a body.

6. Where the Revenant Walks: History, Folklore, and the Arts

A. Medieval Europe

The revenant is a documented medieval belief, not merely a literary invention. The twelfth-century English chronicler William of Newburgh, in his Historia rerum Anglicarum, records several revenant episodes — most famously a man of Berwick who rose from the dead and terrorised his town until the villagers exhumed and burned the body, and the tale of a revenant haunting Melrose Abbey. These accounts treat the revenant as a physical, dangerous nuisance to be dealt with by consecrated ground, dismemberment, or fire — exactly the draugr-logic of the Norse sources.

B. Iceland and Scandinavia

The draugr of the Icelandic sagas is the North’s revenant: it guards its grave-mound, swells to monstrous size, and must be wrestled and beheaded. See Grettis saga (Glamr) and the Eyrbyggja saga (the restless Þórólfr, whose corpse continues to trouble the household until it is moved).

C. The Modern Revenant

  • Cinema: The Revenant (2015) is a useful test case. The title is not about the undead: Hugh Glass is a revenant in the root sense — a man who comes back from near-death and from the wilderness. The film thereby recovers the word’s plain, non-supernatural meaning.
  • Games and fantasy: Magic: The Gathering has printed cards named Revenant; Dark Souls and Elder Scrolls use draugr; the term now proliferates in role-playing games as a category of corporeal undead.
  • Figurative journalism: “a revenant from the 1980s,” “the revenant of an old scandal” — applied to people, policies, and ideas that return unbidden.

7. Figurative and Attributive Uses

Revenant has escaped the graveyard. Its figurative deployment is elegant and slightly ominous, and it is best used sparingly.

Usage Example Effect
A person returned “A revenant from the pre-war cabinet.” Suggests a figure who should have stayed buried
An idea returned “The revenant of inflationary thinking.” Frames an old doctrine as something undead
Adjectival (literary) “a revenant figure in the doorway.” Archaic colouring; a deliberate gothic note
Collective “a country of revenants.” Suggests a whole society of the unburied

Caution: because the word is uncommon, it lands hard. Where ghost or return would be plain, revenant supplies literary weight and a hint of menace — a good reason to use it once, deliberately, and never as decoration.

8. Confusables and Spelling Traps

The most common errors with this word are spelling and look-alike confusion, not misuse.

Wrong Right Why
revenenet revenant A non-word; it fuses revenant with the -net of cabinet or internet. The ending is -ant, the French present-participle suffix.
revennant revenant French has revenir (one n); the doubling is a phantom.
revenent revenant -ent and -ant are both real suffixes, but this word takes -ant.
relevant (in this context) revenant The two are not interchangeable and not related.
reverent revenant Reverent = feeling reverence (from revereri, “to revere”). A reverent ghost is a polite one — a different word.
remnant revenant Remnant = a surviving fragment (from remanere, “to remain”). A different root and a different meaning.
reliant revenant Reliant = dependent (from religare, “to bind”).

The Look-Alike Set at a Glance

Word Root Meaning
Revenant venire — to come One who returns (from the dead)
Relevant levare — to raise Bearing on the matter at hand
Reverent vereri — to revere Showing deep respect
Remnant manere — to remain A leftover fragment
Reliant ligare — to bind Depending on
Remanent manere — to remain Residual (archaic, technical)

Memory hook: a revenant comes back; a reverent admires; a relevant matters; a remnant is what’s left. The roots differ in the middle — and so does the meaning.

9. Model Sentences

“William of Newburgh’s revenant was no ghost: witnesses insisted the man walked in the flesh, and the only remedy was fire. The saga’s draugr was its Norse cousin; the Haitian zombie its descendant without a will; and the cinema’s shambling horde its debased heir. Yet when the aging statesman returned to the chamber, journalists reached past all of them for the older, plainer word: a revenant from a buried decade.”

1. Etymologically, “revenant” means: A) one who is revered B) one who comes back C) one who remains D) one who is relevant

2. Which word shares the venire (“to come”) root with “revenant”? A) revert B) remnant C) revenue D) reverent

3. What most precisely distinguishes a revenant from a ghost? A) A revenant is friendly. B) A revenant returns in bodily form; a ghost is disembodied. C) A revenant cannot speak. D) There is no difference.

4. Which word describes a reanimated corpse without will? A) revenant B) specter C) zombie D) wraith

5. The Norse equivalent of the revenant, guarding its grave-mound in the sagas, is the: A) poltergeist B) banshee C) doppelgänger D) draugr

6. Which sentence uses the word correctly? A) The accountant was a revenant man who respected his elders. B) The village feared the drowned sailor, who returned as a revenant. C) She was revenant to the details of the contract. D) Only a revenant of the evidence survived the flood.

7. The phrase “revert back” is criticized because: A) Revert is informal B) Revert already contains the sense “back,” so back is redundant C) It belongs to genetics only D) Revert means “to come”

Answer Key & Rationale

  1. B. From French revenir, the present participle of “to come back.”
  2. C. Revenue descends from the same revenir/venire root; revert and remnant use vertere and manere, and reverent uses vereri.
  3. B. The revenant’s defining trait is bodily return; a ghost is a disembodied spirit.
  4. C. The zombie is the corpse without autonomous will, in Vodou-derived and cinematic tradition.
  5. D. The draugr is the Norse/Icelandic undead of the sagas.
  6. B. Only B uses the word in its established sense. A confuses revenant with reverent; C confuses it with relevant; D confuses it with remnant.
  7. B. Re- already signals return, so revert back is a redundancy (compare return back, recur back).

## 1. Revenant — Definition and Pronunciation

**Pronunciation:** /ˈrɛv.ə.nənt/. Stress falls on the **first** syllable in English (unlike French, where it falls at the end). The final syllable is weak — *REV-uh-nunt*, not *rev-uh-NANT*.

**Definition (noun):** a person who has **returned from the dead**, especially one who returns in bodily form; more loosely, anyone who returns after a long absence.

**Definition (adjective, archaic/literary):** returning, coming back — *"a revenant spirit."*

* *"The village believed the drowned man had become a **revenant**."*
* *"He came back into public life like a **revenant** from a discredited era."*
* *"The novel is haunted by **revenants** — the unquiet dead and the unwelcome past alike."*

**Register:** literary, historical, and folklore studies; in contemporary prose it often carries a deliberate **archaizing** or **gothic** flavour. It is the precise term where *zombie* would be anachronistic and *ghost* would be wrong.

> **The word's distinctive claim:** a revenant **returns bodily**. A ghost may be a breath of cold air; a revenant is a corpse that walks back through the door. That physicality is the semantic core.

## 2. The *venire* Family: Return, Arrival, and Coming-to-Mind

Because *venire* ("to come") is one of the most productive roots in the Latin inheritance, *revenant* sits inside a large etymological clan. Seeing it whole makes the word easier to fix in memory.

| **advenire** | *ad-* "to" | *avenir* | **advent**, **adventure**, **avenue** |
| **convenire** | *con-* "together" | *convenir* | **convene**, **convention**, **covenant** |

### Two Revelations in This Table

1. **Revenue is a cousin of revenant.** *Revenue* descends from the past participle of *revenir* — literally *"that which has returned,"* i.e. income coming back to the one who is owed it. The same root that gives us a walking corpse gives us a tax term — a memorable illustration of how far a root can travel.
2. **Souvenir is a cousin too.** *Subvenire* meant "to come up (into the mind)," and French *souvenir* retains that sense of memory. A *souvenir* is a memory-object: something that **comes back to you**.

> **Going deeper:** the *ven-* forms also surface in **venue** (a place to which people *come*), **avenue** (a way of *coming* to a place), and **provenance/provenience** (origin — where a thing *comes from*). The root runs through English like a road.

## 3. Revert, Reversion, and Revertant — Cousins by Meaning, Not Root

A word of caution about a word that *looks* like family:

* **Revert** comes from Latin **revertere** = *re-* + *vertere* ("to turn"), not *venire* ("to come"). It means **to turn back** — to return to a former state, practice, or owner.
* **Reversion** (noun): a return to a previous state; also, in law, the return of property to its original owner or the owner's heirs (a *reversionary interest*).
* **Revertant** (noun, genetics): an organism or cell that has **reverted** — a mutant that regains the wild-type phenotype by a second mutation.

| Word | Root | Meaning |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Revenant** | *venire* "come" | One who comes back, especially from the dead |
| **Revert** | *vertere* "turn" | Turn back to a former state; return to a former owner |
| **Revertant** | *vertere* "turn" | A mutant that has reverted (genetics) |
| **Reversion** | *vertere* "turn" | The act of returning; a legal right of return |

> **Usage trap — "revert back":** because *revert* already contains "back" in its *re-*, the phrase *revert back* is a redundancy that careful writers avoid. *"The system **reverted** to its default"* — not *"reverted back to its default."* The same logic condemns *return back*, *recur back*, and *reiterate again*.

| **Banshee** | Irish *bean sídhe* (woman of the fairy mound) | A female spirit whose **wail** announces a death; not an apparition so much as an omen |
| **Doppelgänger** | German (double + goer) | An apparition of a **living** person; a double |
| **Lemure** | Latin *lemures* (restless ghosts) | In Roman belief, the unquiet dead of the household; the animal *lemur* is named after them |
| **Wendigo** | Algonquian | A cannibalistic spirit of the northern forests; not a revenant, but a hunger-spirit |
| **Ghost light / corpse candle** | — | Folkloric lights that precede or follow a death |

> **Etymological footnote:** *ghoul*, *poltergeist*, and *doppelgänger* are reminders that the undead lexicon is one of English's most **borrowed** semantic fields. Where Germanic and Celtic terms mostly name the *appearance* (ghost, shade, fetch), Semitic, German, and Norse terms name the *behaviour* (grave-robbing, noise-making, walking).

---

## 5. Revenant vs. Zombie vs. Ghost: The Three-Way Distinction

This is the distinction most worth internalising, because careless writing blurs it.

| | **Revenant** | **Zombie** | **Ghost** |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **What returns** | The body | The body | The soul |
| **Will** | Retains personal intent (often vengeful) | Usually none; driven by hunger or a controller | Retains personality and memory |
| **Origin** | European folklore: unquiet dead, excommunicated, oath-breakers | Haitian Vodou, then Hollywood | Universal |
| **Register** | Literary, historical, gothic | Popular, cinematic, figurative | Everyday, neutral |
| **Figurative use** | *"a revenant of the past"* — a person or idea returning after long absence | *"zombie company," "zombie idea"* — something dead that keeps operating | *"ghost of a chance," "ghosting someone"* |

* **Revenant** is the word for **bodily return with purpose**.
* **Zombie** is the word for **bodily return without will**.
* **Ghost** is the word for **return without a body**.

---

## 6. Where the Revenant Walks: History, Folklore, and the Arts

### A. Medieval Europe

The revenant is a **documented medieval belief**, not merely a literary invention. The twelfth-century English chronicler **William of Newburgh**, in his *Historia rerum Anglicarum*, records several revenant episodes — most famously a man of **Berwick** who rose from the dead and terrorised his town until the villagers exhumed and burned the body, and the tale of a revenant haunting **Melrose Abbey**. These accounts treat the revenant as a **physical, dangerous nuisance** to be dealt with by consecrated ground, dismemberment, or fire — exactly the draugr-logic of the Norse sources.

### B. Iceland and Scandinavia

The **draugr** of the Icelandic sagas is the North's revenant: it guards its grave-mound, swells to monstrous size, and must be wrestled and beheaded. See *Grettis saga* (Glamr) and the *Eyrbyggja saga* (the restless Þórólfr, whose corpse continues to trouble the household until it is moved).

### C. The Modern Revenant

* **Cinema:** *The Revenant* (2015) is a useful test case. The title is not about the undead: Hugh Glass is a **revenant** in the root sense — a man who **comes back** from near-death and from the wilderness. The film thereby recovers the word's plain, non-supernatural meaning.
* **Games and fantasy:** *Magic: The Gathering* has printed cards named *Revenant*; *Dark Souls* and *Elder Scrolls* use *draugr*; the term now proliferates in role-playing games as a category of corporeal undead.
* **Figurative journalism:** *"a revenant from the 1980s,"* *"the revenant of an old scandal"* — applied to people, policies, and ideas that return unbidden.

---

## 7. Figurative and Attributive Uses

*Revenant* has escaped the graveyard. Its figurative deployment is **elegant and slightly ominous**, and it is best used sparingly.

| Usage | Example | Effect |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **A person returned** | *"A **revenant** from the pre-war cabinet."* | Suggests a figure who should have stayed buried |
| **An idea returned** | *"The **revenant** of inflationary thinking."* | Frames an old doctrine as something undead |
| **Adjectival (literary)** | *"a **revenant** figure in the doorway."* | Archaic colouring; a deliberate gothic note |
| **Collective** | *"a country of **revenants**."* | Suggests a whole society of the unburied |

**Caution:** because the word is uncommon, it lands hard. Where *ghost* or *return* would be plain, *revenant* supplies **literary weight and a hint of menace** — a good reason to use it once, deliberately, and never as decoration.

## 8. Confusables and Spelling Traps

The most common errors with this word are **spelling** and **look-alike confusion**, not misuse.

| Wrong | Right | Why |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| ~~revenenet~~ | **revenant** | A non-word; it fuses *revenant* with the *-net* of *cabinet* or *internet*. The ending is **-ant**, the French present-participle suffix. |
| ~~revennant~~ | **revenant** | French has *revenir* (one *n*); the doubling is a phantom. |
| ~~revenent~~ | **revenant** | *-ent* and *-ant* are both real suffixes, but this word takes **-ant**. |
| ~~relevant~~ (in this context) | **revenant** | The two are **not** interchangeable and not related. |
| ~~reverent~~ | **revenant** | *Reverent* = feeling reverence (from *revereri*, "to revere"). A reverent ghost is a polite one — a different word. |
| ~~remnant~~ | **revenant** | *Remnant* = a surviving fragment (from *remanere*, "to remain"). A different root and a different meaning. |
| ~~reliant~~ | **revenant** | *Reliant* = dependent (from *religare*, "to bind"). |

### The Look-Alike Set at a Glance

| Word | Root | Meaning |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Revenant** | *venire* — to come | One who returns (from the dead) |
| **Relevant** | *levare* — to raise | Bearing on the matter at hand |
| **Reverent** | *vereri* — to revere | Showing deep respect |
| **Remnant** | *manere* — to remain | A leftover fragment |
| **Reliant** | *ligare* — to bind | Depending on |
| **Remanent** | *manere* — to remain | Residual (archaic, technical) |

> **Memory hook:** a re**v**e**n**ant comes **back**; a reve**r**ent **admires**; a re**lev**ant **matters**; a re**mn**ant is what's **left**. The roots differ in the middle — and so does the meaning.

## 9. Model Sentences

> *"William of Newburgh's **revenant** was no **ghost**: witnesses insisted the man walked in the **flesh**, and the only remedy was fire. The saga's **draugr** was its Norse cousin; the Haitian **zombie** its descendant without a will; and the cinema's shambling horde its debased heir. Yet when the aging statesman returned to the chamber, journalists reached past all of them for the older, plainer word: a **revenant** from a buried decade."*

## 10. Knowledge Retention Quiz

**1. Etymologically, "revenant" means:**
A) one who is revered
B) one who comes back
C) one who remains
D) one who is relevant

**2. Which word shares the *venire* ("to come") root with "revenant"?**
A) revert
B) remnant
C) revenue
D) reverent

**3. What most precisely distinguishes a revenant from a ghost?**
A) A revenant is friendly.
B) A revenant returns in bodily form; a ghost is disembodied.
C) A revenant cannot speak.
D) There is no difference.

**4. Which word describes a reanimated corpse *without will*?**
A) revenant
B) specter
C) zombie
D) wraith

**5. The Norse equivalent of the revenant, guarding its grave-mound in the sagas, is the:**
A) poltergeist
B) banshee
C) doppelgänger
D) draugr

**6. Which sentence uses the word correctly?**
A) The accountant was a revenant man who respected his elders.
B) The village feared the drowned sailor, who returned as a revenant.
C) She was revenant to the details of the contract.
D) Only a revenant of the evidence survived the flood.

**7. The phrase "revert back" is criticized because:**
A) *Revert* is informal
B) *Revert* already contains the sense "back," so *back* is redundant
C) It belongs to genetics only
D) *Revert* means "to come"

### Answer Key & Rationale
1. **B.** From French *revenir*, the present participle of "to come back."
2. **C.** *Revenue* descends from the same *revenir*/*venire* root; *revert* and *remnant* use *vertere* and *manere*, and *reverent* uses *vereri*.
3. **B.** The revenant's defining trait is **bodily** return; a ghost is a disembodied spirit.
4. **C.** The zombie is the corpse without autonomous will, in Vodou-derived and cinematic tradition.
5. **D.** The *draugr* is the Norse/Icelandic undead of the sagas.
6. **B.** Only B uses the word in its established sense. A confuses *revenant* with *reverent*; C confuses it with *relevant*; D confuses it with *remnant*.
7. **B.** *Re-* already signals return, so *revert back* is a redundancy (compare *return back*, *recur back*).
