Introduction: The Semantics of Continuation

English is extraordinarily rich in verbs meaning “to keep going.” This abundance is not redundancy — each word encodes a distinct attitude toward the act of continuing: whether it is easy or hard, voluntary or forced, neutral or heroic, informal or legalistic. Choosing correctly is a matter of register and connotation, not mere synonyms.

                    [ THE CONTINUATION SPECTRUM ]

  Neutral ───────────────────────────────────────────────► Adversarial
  continue        proceed     carry on    persist    press on    soldier on
  (general)       (formal)    (informal)  (stubborn) (determined) (heroic)

1. Continue (Verb)

Phonetics: /kənˈtɪnjuː/.

Etymological Architecture

From Latin continuare (to join together, make continuous), from continuus (“uninterrupted”).

Semantic Core

The neutral, default choice. It means to keep doing something, or to resume after an interruption, with no emotional colouring.

Syntactic Patterns

  • continue + to-infinitive: “She continued to work through the night.”
  • continue + gerund: “She continued working through the night.”
  • continue + noun (transitive): “They continued the investigation.”
  • continue (intransitive): “The rain continued for days.”

Nuance: continue to do can subtly suggest a fresh or deliberate continuation; continue doing emphasizes uninterrupted persistence. The difference is slight.

Model Sentence: “Despite the objections, the committee continued its deliberations until dawn.”

2. Carry On (Phrasal Verb, chiefly BrE)

Semantic Core

Informal, sturdy, and distinctively British. To carry on is to continue an activity despite difficulty, interruption, or objection — often in the spirit of “keep calm and carry on.”

Syntactic Patterns

  • carry on + gerund: “They carried on talking after the interruption.”
  • carry on with + noun: “Let’s carry on with the meeting.”
  • carry on (intransitive): “The work carried on as usual.”

The Complication: A Second Sense

In British colloquial English, carry on also means to behave in a foolish, excited, or improper way: * “The children were carrying on all morning.” (= misbehaving) * “There was a right carry-on at the office.” (noun: a fuss or commotion)

Model Sentence: “The power failed, but the surgeons carried on by torchlight.”

3. Go On (Phrasal Verb)

Semantic Core

Informal and extremely flexible. Its meanings diverge sharply depending on what follows:

Structure Meaning Example
go on + gerund Continue the same activity “He went on talking.” (kept talking)
go on + to-infinitive Move on to a new activity “He went on to discuss the budget.” (turned to it next)
go on (intransitive) Continue; happen “The party went on until midnight.”
go on about Talk at tedious length “She went on about her holiday for an hour.”
go on! Encouragement “Go on, you can do it!”

Critical nuance: go on doing vs. go on to do is a classic exam distinction. Doing = same action continues; to do = transition to a different action.

Model Sentence: “She went on to explain the theory, having first gone on writing the equations on the board.”

4. Proceed (Verb)

Phonetics: /prəˈsiːd/.

Etymological Architecture

Latin prōcedere = prō- (“forward”) + cēdere (“to go”). Literally: “to go forward.”

Semantic Core

Formal and procedural. Proceed means to continue after a pause or to move forward along a planned sequence. It is the natural verb of meetings, law, ceremonies, and instructions.

Syntactic Patterns

  • proceed with + noun: “We will now proceed with the vote.”
  • proceed to + infinitive: “The committee proceeded to review the evidence.”
  • proceed (intransitive): “Please proceed to Gate 12.”

Register note: In conversation, proceed can sound stiff or bureaucratic — “Do proceed” — which is precisely why it suits legal and formal contexts.

Model Sentence: “Having verified the signatures, the notary proceeded to certify the deed.”

5. Persist (Verb)

Phonetics: /pəˈsɪst/.

Etymological Architecture

Latin persistere = per- (“through, thoroughly”) + sistere (“to stand”). Literally: “to keep standing.”

Semantic Core

To continue firmly and often stubbornly, especially in the face of opposition, difficulty, or discouragement. Unlike continue, persist carries the connotation of resistance — and can be either admirable or irritating.

Syntactic Patterns

  • persist in + gerund (frequently pejorative): “He persisted in asking questions.”
  • persist with + noun: “She persisted with her research despite the setbacks.”
  • persist (intransitive, of things): “The smell persisted for days.”

Nuance: Persist vs. Persevere

  • Persist can imply stubbornness, even against reason: “He persisted in his error.”
  • Persevere is almost always positive — continuing toward a worthy goal with steady determination: “She persevered and earned her degree.”

Model Sentence: “The anomaly persisted across every trial, so the team persevered until they identified its cause.”

6. Keep On (Phrasal Verb, Informal)

Semantic Core

An emphatic, informal version of continue, stressing repetition and endurance: * “She kept on working despite the pain.” * “The alarm kept on ringing.”

The “Nag” Idiom

  • keep on at someone = to pester or nag:
    • “His mother kept on at him to tidy his room.”

Nuance: keep doing and keep on doing are close, but keep on adds insistence or weariness.

7. Press On / Push On (Phrasal Verbs)

Semantic Core

To continue resolutely in the face of difficulty, often physical exhaustion. Slightly heroic or narrative in tone: * “Exhausted, the climbers pressed on through the blizzard.” * “We must push on; there is no time to rest.”

Register: Literary, motivational, journalistic. Common in narration of journeys, campaigns, and crises.

8. Soldier On (Phrasal Verb, BrE, Informal)

Semantic Core

To continue doggedly despite hardship or adversity, often with the connotation of uncomplaining endurance: * “The team soldiered on after losing its captain.” * “She soldiered on through chemotherapy.”

Register: Informal British, mildly affectionate or admiring. Implies quiet fortitude rather than triumphant heroism.

9. Resume (Verb)

Phonetics: /rɪˈzjuːm/.

Etymological Architecture

Latin resumere = re- (“again”) + sumere (“to take up”). Literally: “to take up again.”

Semantic Core

To begin again after an interruption — the opposite of merely continuing without a break. Formal register: * “Negotiations will resume tomorrow morning.” * “She resumed her seat.” * “The music resumed.”

Nuance: Resume presupposes a break; continue does not necessarily.

10. Endure (Verb)

Phonetics: /ɪnˈdjʊə/.

Etymological Architecture

Latin indurare = in- (“in”) + durus (“hard”). Literally: “to make hard” — to harden oneself to suffering.

Semantic Core

To suffer patiently and to continue to exist over time: * “They endured months of hardship.” * “The custom endures to this day.”

Nuance: Endure adds a dimension of suffering borne that continue lacks entirely.

11. The Master Comparison Matrix

Verb Register Transitivity Connotation Typical Pattern
Continue Neutral Trans. & intr. Plain continuation continue to do / doing
Carry on Informal (BrE) Intr. / + with Resolute, plucky carry on doing / with
Go on Informal Intr. Flexible; same vs. next go on doing / to do
Proceed Formal Intr. / + with Procedural, forward proceed with / to do
Persist Neutral/formal Intr. / + in Stubborn, insistent persist in doing
Keep on Informal Intr. Emphatic repetition keep on doing
Press on Semi-formal Intr. Determined, heroic press on
Soldier on Informal (BrE) Intr. Uncomplaining fortitude soldier on
Resume Formal Trans. & intr. Restart after a break resume (doing)
Endure Semi-formal Trans. & intr. Bear suffering; last endure hardship

12. Prepositional and Structural Traps

  • go on doing (same activity) ⟷ go on to do (next activity). The single biggest source of confusion.
  • persist in + gerund — not persist to do: ✅ “persist in complaining.”
  • proceed with + noun / proceed to + verb: ✅ “proceed with the plan” / “proceed to sign.”
  • carry on with + noun, or carry on + gerund: ✅ “carry on with your work” / “carry on working.”
  • resume takes a break: ❌ “resume the same activity without stopping.”

13. Diagnostic Application: Synthesizing the Lexicon

“The negotiations had stalled at midnight, but the mediator urged both delegations to carry on. They proceeded to exchange revised proposals; one side persisted in demanding concessions the other refused to grant. Tempers frayed, yet the talks went on — not because anyone expected a breakthrough, but because neither side dared be the first to leave. At dawn they resumed the formal session, and the exhausted envoys soldiered on, knowing the alternative was collapse.”

14. Knowledge Retention Quiz

1. Which verb presupposes an interruption before continuing? A) continue B) persist C) resume D) keep on

2. What is the difference between “go on doing” and “go on to do”? A) No difference. B) “Go on doing” continues the same activity; “go on to do” moves to a new one. C) “Go on doing” is formal; “go on to do” is informal. D) “Go on to do” means to stop.

3. Which verb carries a connotation of stubbornness and often irritation? A) continue B) persist C) resume D) proceed

4. “The team ___ on despite losing its captain.” Which fits the British informal, uncomplaining tone? A) proceeded B) persisted C) soldiered D) resumed

5. Which sentence uses the correct preposition? A) She persisted to ask questions. B) She persisted in asking questions. C) She persisted on asking questions. D) She persisted for asking questions.

Answer Key & Rationale

  1. C. Resume means to begin again after a break; the others do not require one.
  2. B. The gerund keeps the same action; the infinitive signals a transition to a new action.
  3. B. Persist often carries a pejorative, stubborn nuance (especially persist in doing).
  4. C. Soldier on is the informal British phrase for uncomplaining endurance.
  5. B. Persist governs the preposition in followed by a gerund.

## 12. Prepositional and Structural Traps
