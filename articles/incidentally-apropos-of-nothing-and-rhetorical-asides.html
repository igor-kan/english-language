Introduction: The Metadiscursive Pivot

Human communication rarely follows a strictly linear trajectory. In any serious intellectual dialogue, negotiation, or narrative prose, the speaker frequently needs to step outside the primary argument to inject a secondary observation, drop a historical footnote, introduce an unexpected fact, or execute a deliberate conversational detour.

In linguistics, words and phrases that manage these transitions are known as discourse markers of digression (or parenthetical disjuncts). According to Paul Grice’s foundational Maxim of Relation (“Be relevant”), any unannounced leap to an unrelated or subordinate topic threatens the coherence of a conversation.

Discourse markers such as incidentally, be it said incidentally, and apropos of nothing function as conversational shock absorbers: they explicitly signal to the listener the precise logical, hierarchical, and stylistic relationship between the main thesis and the incoming tangent.

                     ┌── Associative Tangent  ──> "Incidentally" / "By the way"
                     │
Rhetorical Asides ───┼── Complete Non Sequitur ──> "Apropos of nothing" / "Out of the blue"
 (Metadiscourse)     │
                     ├── Formal Subordination ──> "Be it said incidentally" / "In passing"
                     │
                     └── High-Register Gloss  ──> "En passant" / "Obiter dictum"

1. Deconstructing “Incidentally”

Etymology and Morphological Roots

  • Origin: From Latin incidentem (nominative incidens), present participle of incidere—formed from in- (“upon”, “into”) and cadere (“to fall”). Literally, that which “falls upon” or happens concurrently with a primary event.
  • Morphological Family: incident (noun), incidental (adjective), incidentally (adverb), incidence (rate of occurrence).

Phonetics & Common Orthographic Errors

  • Phonetics: /ˌɪnsɪˈdentli/ or /ˌɪnsəˈdentəli/
  • Orthographic Trap: Writers occasionally misspell the word as \(\times\) incidently. In standard English, the adverb is formed from the adjective incidental by adding -ly, yielding incidentally.

Dual Syntactic Behaviors

The word operates in two fundamentally different grammatical capacities:

A. As a Sentence Adverb / Discourse Disjunct (The Aside)

In this role, incidentally modifies the entire clause, signaling that the upcoming proposition is supplementary, secondary, or associative: * “We have finalized the Q3 server infrastructure budget. Incidentally, the chief architect requested additional telemetry nodes for the distributed cluster.” * “She is an authority on early Roman jurisprudence—and, incidentally, an accomplished cellist.”

B. As an Adverb of Manner / Secondary Cause

Here, incidentally means as a chance consequence, subordinately, or not primarily by deliberate design: * “The security sandbox was engineered to isolate microservices, but it incidentally improved cache hit rates across all worker threads.” * “The economic policy benefited large exporters, but only incidentally.”

2. The Formal Construction: “Be It Said Incidentally”

Syntactic Architecture

The phrase “be it said incidentally” is a classical subjunctive passive formula (“let it be said incidentally” or “may it be observed in passing”).

In traditional grammar, it functions as an absolute parenthetical clause: \[\text{Matrix Clause} \longleftrightarrow \text{[} \mathbf{Be\ it\ said\ incidentally} \text{]} \longleftrightarrow \text{Resumed Exposition}\]

"The treaty of 1648—[be it said incidentally]—reshaped the sovereignty of the German principalities."
                           │
                           └── Subjunctive copula "be" + passive participle "said"

Register and Pragmatic Function

  • Register: High formal, academic, historiographical, or legal prose.
  • Function: It allows a scholar or essayist to introduce an explanatory nuance, caveat, or historical footnote without committing to making that nuance a major theme of the exposition.
  • Tone: Elegant, measured, dispassionate, and aristocratic in intellectual posture.

Exemplars in Context

  • “His personal contribution to the mathematical proof was modest—be it said incidentally, though without malice—compared to Gauss’s monumental breakthroughs.”
  • “The committee approved the capital allocation—be it said incidentally that two senior directors abstained from the vote.”
  • “The manuscript—be it said incidentally to his greater philosophical works—reveals an intimate portrait of daily life in eighteenth-century Edinburgh.”
NoteParallel Formulaic Siblings

English contains a family of related subjunctive parentheticals sharing this exact syntactic template: * “Be it remembered that…” (Admonishing the reader not to overlook a vital historical fact). * “Be it noted that…” (Directing analytical attention to a technical prerequisite). * “Be it understood that…” (Setting clear boundaries of interpretation).

3. “Apropos of Nothing” vs. “Apropos of…”

Few idiomatic phrases illustrate the sensitivity of English prepositions better than the contrast between apropos of [something] and apropos of nothing.

Etymological Foundation

  • Origin: From French à propos, literally meaning “to the purpose”, “fit”, “seasonable”, or “aptly timed”.
  • Core Sense: Relevancy and contextual alignment.

The Divergent Polarities

  "Apropos of [Topic]"                    "Apropos of nothing"
┌─────────────────────────────────┐     ┌─────────────────────────────────┐
│ • Relevant & Connected          │     │ • Complete Non Sequitur         │
│ • "With regard to..."           │     │ • Totally unprompted            │
│ • Logical transition marker     │     │ • Spontaneous / Out of nowhere  │
└─────────────────────────────────┘     └─────────────────────────────────┘

A. “Apropos of [Noun / Pronoun]” (The Bridge of Relevance)

Used as a formal prepositional locution meaning with respect to, concerning, or on the subject of: * “Apropos of our recent discussion on distributed consensus algorithms, have you evaluated the Raft protocol’s performance under asymmetric network partitions?” * “The chairman’s remarks were particularly apropos of the ongoing merger negotiations.”

B. “Apropos of Nothing” (The Deliberate Non Sequitur)

Used when a speaker introduces a remark that has absolutely zero connection to what was previously being discussed. It acknowledges that the statement is unprompted, spontaneous, or bursting out of internal contemplation rather than conversational flow: * “We were quietly reviewing database schemas when Marcus announced, apropos of nothing, that he was considering moving to Patagonia.” * “Apropos of nothing, did you know that honey never spoils?” * “She stared at the rain and murmured, apropos of nothing, that life was too short for bad coffee.”

Rhetorical and Psychological Implications

When someone speaks apropos of nothing, it frequently betrays an underlying psychological state: 1. Preoccupation: The speaker’s mind has been fixated on a private thought that suddenly breaches the surface of consciousness. 2. Nervous Deflection: Squeezing in a difficult or awkward subject by abandoning the current conversational thread entirely. 3. Conversational Whimsy: Deliberately injecting spontaneity into an otherwise predictable dialogue.

5. Lexical Deep Dives: Sophisticated Equivalents

A. “En Passant” (The Chess Metaphor)

  • Phonetics: /ˌɒ̃ pæˈsɒ̃/ (approx. on pass-on)
  • Literal Sense: French for “in passing”.
  • Usage: Borrowed both from standard French and the chess rule en passant (capturing a pawn that has advanced two squares). In prose, it implies addressing something fleetingly, deftly, or almost nonchalantly while on the way to a larger goal:
    • “In his introductory chapter, the author mentions en passant that classical mechanics is merely a low-velocity approximation of relativity.”
    • “He dropped the revelation en passant, as if it were of no consequence whatever.”

B. “Parenthetically”

  • Syntactic Role: Adverb acting as a metadiscursive signal.
  • Sense: Explicitly tells the reader that what follows is analogous to text enclosed within typographical brackets—informative context that can be omitted without harming the core grammatical syntax:
    • “The algorithm performs with logarithmic efficiency (parenthetically, assuming uniform key distribution across all hash buckets).”
    • “He noted, parenthetically, that previous attempts to reproduce the experiment had encountered equipment anomalies.”

C. “Obiter Dictum” (Plural: Obiter Dicta)

  • Etymology: Latin obiter (“by the way”, “in passing”) + dictum (“saying”, “word”).
  • Jurisprudential Distinction: In common law, a judicial opinion consists of two components:
    1. Ratio Decidendi: The legal reasoning essential to the ruling; constitutes binding legal precedent.
    2. Obiter Dictum: Incidental observations, illustrations, or musings made by the judge in the ruling that are not strictly necessary to resolve the dispute; does not create binding precedent, though it may hold persuasive authority.
  • General Rhetorical Extension: Any opinion expressed in passing by an expert or commentator outside the scope of their primary argument:
    • “The professor’s remark regarding the future of quantum computing was a mere obiter dictum, yet it sparked a fierce debate among doctoral candidates.”

6. Syntactic Mechanics: Punctuation and Placement

How a digression is punctuated dictates its rhetorical weight, pacing, and dramatic impact.

1. Commas: The Seamless Transition

Used when the digression is mild, brief, and intended to blend fluidly with the surrounding syntax: * “We should, incidentally, verify the cryptographic signature before committing the transaction.” * “Incidentally, the flight has been rescheduled for Thursday morning.”

2. Em-Dashes (—): The Dramatic Interruption

Em-dashes physically disrupt the visual flow of the sentence, creating suspense or emphasizing the parenthetical nature of the thought: * “The architect’s initial blueprint—be it said incidentally—had omitted the entire emergency stairwell.” * “His latest publication—apropos of nothing in his prior research catalog—explores seventeenth-century Dutch cartography.”

3. Parentheses ( ): The Informational Footnote

Parentheses visually enclose the thought, signaling that it is purely supplementary and can be skipped without loss of narrative continuity: * “The system achieved five-nines availability last quarter (incidentally, our peak concurrency reached forty thousand requests per second).”

4. Semicolons ;: Binding Two Coordinated Propositions

When incidentally connects two complete independent clauses, it requires a semicolon before it and a comma after it: * \(\times\) Run-on / Comma Splice: “The compiler detected three deprecated calls, incidentally they have all been refactored.” * \(\checkmark\) Correct: “The compiler detected three deprecated calls; incidentally, they have all been refactored.”

7. The Rhetorical Strategy: The “Trojan Horse” Aside

While discourse markers of digression ostensibly announce minor points, astute rhetoricians, negotiators, and politicians frequently use them as subversive instruments of persuasion.

                     [ THE "TROJAN HORSE" ASIDE ]

       Speaker Downplays Weight:      "Incidentally..." / "By the way..."
                                              │
       Listener Lowers Defenses:      "This is just a casual comment."
                                              │
       Critical Payload Delivered:    [The single most crucial concession,
                                       damaging admission, or demand]

The Negotiator’s Gambit (“Columbo Technique”)

Named after the fictional television detective Lieutenant Columbo, who would exit a suspect’s room only to turn back at the door and say: “Oh, just one more thing… by the way…” * By framing a question or demand as an afterthought (“Incidentally, did you confirm the warranty indemnification clause?”), the speaker prevents the counterparty from preparing defensive barriers.

The Diplomatic Softener

Directly stating an uncomfortable truth can trigger defensiveness. Wrapping it in an aside softens the impact: * Blunt: “Your unit missed the delivery deadline.” * Softened Aside: “We reviewed the quarterly deliverables yesterday; incidentally, the telemetry report from your team appears to have been delayed.”

Summary of Best Practices

  1. Match the Register: Use by the way for conversation; reserve incidentally, be it said incidentally, and en passant for analytical essays, formal debate, or executive memoranda.
  2. Respect the Preposition: Never say apropos to or apropos of nothing when you mean apropos of [a topic].
  3. Punctuate with Precision: Always set off parenthetical discourse markers with appropriate commas, em-dashes, or semicolons to prevent comma splices.
  4. Be Intentional with Digressions: Use them sparingly to illuminate, contextualize, or tactically soften, rather than to clutter your prose.

```text
       Listener Lowers Defenses:      "This is just a casual comment."
                                              │
       Critical Payload Delivered:    [The single most crucial concession,
                                       damaging admission, or demand]
```

### The Negotiator's Gambit ("Columbo Technique")
Named after the fictional television detective Lieutenant Columbo, who would exit a suspect's room only to turn back at the door and say: *"Oh, just one more thing... by the way..."*
* By framing a question or demand as an afterthought (*"Incidentally, did you confirm the warranty indemnification clause?"*), the speaker prevents the counterparty from preparing defensive barriers.

### The Diplomatic Softener
Directly stating an uncomfortable truth can trigger defensiveness. Wrapping it in an aside softens the impact:
* **Blunt:** *"Your unit missed the delivery deadline."*
* **Softened Aside:** *"We reviewed the quarterly deliverables yesterday; **incidentally**, the telemetry report from your team appears to have been delayed."*

## 8. Diagnostic Decision Guide

When choosing how to pivot away from your main argument, refer to this selection matrix:

```
                                [ Nature of the Digression ]
                                             │
                       Does it have any connection to the topic?
                                    ╱            ╲
                                  YES             NO
                                  ╱                 ╲
                    Is it formal or literary?      [ Complete Non Sequitur ]
                           ╱        ╲              • "Apropos of nothing"
                         YES         NO            • "Out of the blue"
                         ╱             ╲
              [ High Register ]    [ Casual / Direct ]
              • "Be it said        • "Incidentally"
                incidentally"      • "By the way"
              • "En passant"       • "Speaking of which"
              • "Parenthetically"
```

### Summary of Best Practices
1. **Match the Register:** Use *by the way* for conversation; reserve *incidentally*, *be it said incidentally*, and *en passant* for analytical essays, formal debate, or executive memoranda.
2. **Respect the Preposition:** Never say *apropos to* or *apropos of nothing* when you mean *apropos of [a topic]*.
3. **Punctuate with Precision:** Always set off parenthetical discourse markers with appropriate commas, em-dashes, or semicolons to prevent comma splices.
4. **Be Intentional with Digressions:** Use them sparingly to illuminate, contextualize, or tactically soften, rather than to clutter your prose.
