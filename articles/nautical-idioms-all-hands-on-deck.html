Maritime Metaphors: A Masterclass in Nautical Idioms — From ‘All Hands on Deck’ to ‘Loose Cannon’

Charting the semantic currents of English idioms inherited from the age of sail, from collective emergency to reckless unpredictability

Introduction

Few domains have saturated the English lexicon as pervasively as the sea. For three centuries, Britain’s naval and merchant fleets constituted the world’s dominant economic and military force, and the working language of the shipyard, the quarterdeck, and the forecastle quietly leaked into everyday speech. The result is a deep stratum of nautical idioms — fixed expressions whose literal maritime origins have been bleached away, leaving only a vivid figurative residue.

This masterclass anchors itself in one of the most recognizable of these expressions, all hands on deck, and then charts the surrounding semantic archipelago. We will group these idioms by the conceptual work they perform: summoning collective effort, asserting order and competence, describing stability and its disruption, reading authenticity, and capturing shared fate or wasted opportunity.

              [ THE NAUTICAL IDIOM SEASCAPE ]

   Collective Urgency ───► All hands on deck / Batten down the hatches
   Order & Competence ───► Know the ropes / Run a tight ship / Shipshape
   Stability & Disruption► On an even keel / Rock the boat / Loose cannon
   Authenticity & Read   ──► True colors / Cut of one's jib / By and large
   Fate, Opportunity     ──► In the same boat / Miss the boat

Cluster I — Collective Effort, Urgency, and Preparation

1. All Hands on Deck

  • Nautical Origin: In the age of sail, the crew of a warship or merchantman was referred to collectively as hands (able-bodied seamen whose labor powered the vessel). When a ship confronted a squall, an enemy, or a sudden emergency, the bosun’s call would ring out “All hands on deck!” — a command summoning every single crew member, including those off watch, to the open deck to assist.
  • Semantic Core: An urgent mobilization of the entire group. Figuratively, it signals that a situation requires everyone’s immediate, simultaneous contribution; no one is exempt from pitching in.
  • Register: Informal to neutral; common in workplaces, households, and media. It retains a faintly energetic, crisis-driven tone.
  • Single-Word Equivalent: Mobilize, rally, muster.
  • Polar Opposite: Stand down; every man for himself.

Model Sentence: With the product launch forty-eight hours away and the backend still failing load tests, the engineering director declared all hands on deck.

2. Batten Down the Hatches

  • Nautical Origin: A hatch is an opening in a ship’s deck; a batten is a narrow strip of wood or metal. Before heavy weather, sailors covered hatches with tarpaulin and secured the covering with battens nailed into place, sealing the interior against flooding.
  • Semantic Core: To make thorough defensive preparations in anticipation of trouble, danger, or turbulence. It implies proactive hardening rather than reactive scrambling.
  • Register: Neutral to slightly colloquial; frequently deployed in financial, corporate, and household contexts.
  • Single-Word Equivalent: Prepare, fortify, brace for impact.
  • Polar Opposite: Be caught off guard; leave exposed.

Model Sentence: Sensing an impending economic downturn, the CFO ordered every division to batten down the hatches and freeze discretionary spending.

3. Clear the Decks

  • Nautical Origin: Before going into battle, a crew stripped the deck of loose stores, hammocks, and equipment — anything that might obstruct the guns or injure the men — to create an unobstructed fighting space.
  • Semantic Core: To remove obstacles, distractions, or clutter so that a task can be undertaken cleanly. It connotes readiness for action.
  • Register: Neutral; idiomatic in project management and personal organization.
  • Single-Word Equivalent: Prepare, streamline, remove obstructions.
  • Polar Opposite: Clutter; leave impediments in place.

Model Sentence: She spent the morning clearing the decks — closing stale tickets and delegating minor requests — so she could focus on the acquisition due diligence.

4. Weather the Storm (and Ride Out the Storm)

  • Nautical Origin: To weather a storm means to sail through it without being sunk or blown off course; to ride out a storm is to hold position and endure it rather than flee.
  • Semantic Core: To survive a period of extreme difficulty, crisis, or opposition, emerging intact on the other side. It emphasizes endurance rather than avoidance.
  • Register: Neutral to elevated; common in economic and political journalism.
  • Single-Word Equivalent: Endure, survive, withstand.
  • Polar Opposite: Capsize; founder; be overwhelmed.

Model Sentence: The century-old family firm weathered the storm of the pandemic by diversifying into direct-to-consumer logistics.

Cluster II — Competence, Order, and Leadership

5. Know the Ropes (Learn the Ropes / Show Someone the Ropes)

  • Nautical Origin: A square-rigged ship carried miles of rope (rigging) controlling its sails, yards, and masts. An able seaman had to memorize precisely which line did what — hence to know the ropes — while a green newcomer had to learn the ropes from a veteran who would show him the ropes.
  • Semantic Core: To understand the procedures, nuances, and informal rules of a job, organization, or system. It is the idiomatic marker of operational competence.
  • Register: Neutral; ubiquitous in professional onboarding.
  • Single-Word Equivalent: Master the procedure; get oriented; be seasoned.
  • Polar Opposite: Be green; be clueless; flounder.

Model Sentence: It took the new hire a full quarter to learn the ropes of the regulatory filing system, but she now navigates it effortlessly.

6. Run a Tight Ship

  • Nautical Origin: A captain who ran a tight ship kept rigging taut, drills sharp, and discipline strict, producing an efficient and reliable vessel.
  • Semantic Core: To manage an organization with strict discipline, clear standards, and high efficiency. It carries an approving tone, though it can shade into rigidity.
  • Register: Neutral; frequent in business and institutional discourse.
  • Single-Word Equivalent: Manage rigorously; run an efficient operation.
  • Polar Opposite: Run a loose ship; be lax; be disorganized.

Model Sentence: The new principal runs a tight ship, and the school’s attendance and test scores have both climbed accordingly.

7. Shipshape (and Bristol Fashion)

  • Nautical Origin: Shipshape means arranged in the orderly, seaworthy manner of a well-kept vessel. The intensifier Bristol fashion honors the port of Bristol, whose ships were reputed during the 18th and 19th centuries to be kept in immaculate order.
  • Semantic Core: Neat, tidy, organized, and fully prepared. It is the archetypal idiom of external order reflecting internal readiness.
  • Register: Slightly dated and charmingly informal; often used in domestic and workplace settings.
  • Single-Word Equivalent: Tidy, spick-and-span, in good order.
  • Polar Opposite: In disarray; a shambles; helter-skelter.

Model Sentence: By the time the auditors arrived, the bookkeeper had the ledgers shipshape and Bristol fashion.

8. At the Helm (Take the Helm)

  • Nautical Origin: The helm is the wheel or tiller by which a ship is steered. The person at the helm commands the vessel’s course.
  • Semantic Core: In a position of leadership, control, or decision-making. To take the helm is to assume command; at the helm describes ongoing stewardship.
  • Register: Neutral to elevated; common in political and corporate contexts.
  • Single-Word Equivalent: In charge; at the controls; at the reins.
  • Polar Opposite: Below decks; out of the loop; without authority.

Model Sentence: With the founding CEO stepping down, the board debated for months who would take the helm of the embattled conglomerate.

Cluster III — Stability and Its Disruption

9. On an Even Keel

  • Nautical Origin: The keel is a ship’s central structural beam running along its bottom. A vessel floating level — neither listing nor rolling heavily — is said to be on an even keel.
  • Semantic Core: In a state of stability, calm, and emotional or financial equilibrium. It is the idiomatic antonym of chaos and volatility.
  • Register: Neutral; favored in health, finance, and emotional contexts.
  • Single-Word Equivalent: Stable, balanced, steady.
  • Polar Opposite: On an uneven keel; unsteady; listing; in turmoil.

Model Sentence: After a turbulent restructuring, the startup is finally back on an even keel.

10. Rock the Boat

  • Nautical Origin: To rock a small boat is to disturb its stability, risking a capsize and endangering everyone aboard.
  • Semantic Core: To disturb a stable situation by raising objections, making controversial changes, or challenging the status quo. It is often used in the negative (don’t rock the boat).
  • Register: Neutral to informal.
  • Single-Word Equivalent: Disturb the status quo; make waves; cause trouble.
  • Polar Opposite: Keep the peace; go along; steady the ship.

Model Sentence: She decided not to rock the boat during her first month, quietly observing the team’s dynamics before proposing reform.

11. Smooth Sailing (Plain Sailing)

  • Nautical Origin: Plain sailing (originally plane sailing) referred to a simplified navigational method that treated a short stretch of ocean as a flat plane, making course calculations easy. The spelling plain later won out, reinforcing the sense of simple, unobstructed.
  • Semantic Core: Easy, trouble-free progress toward a goal. It frequently appears with a caveat — it’s not all smooth sailing.
  • Register: Neutral to informal.
  • Single-Word Equivalent: Effortless, straightforward, unimpeded.
  • Polar Opposite: Heavy going; rough seas; an uphill battle.

Model Sentence: The migration to the new database was surprisingly smooth sailing, thanks to months of dry-run rehearsal.

12. Loose Cannon

  • Nautical Origin: On wooden warships, cannon weighed several tons and were lashed to the gunports. In battle or heavy seas, a cannon could break free of its restraints and roll unpredictably across the deck, crushing anything — and anyone — in its path.
  • Semantic Core: A person or situation that is dangerously unpredictable, reckless, and beyond control. It describes a liability to one’s own side as much as a threat to others.
  • Register: Neutral; widely used in politics, media, and management.
  • Single-Word Equivalent: Wildcard; loose wire; uncontrollable element.
  • Polar Opposite: Steady hand; team player; reliable operator.

Model Sentence: The charismatic but erratic spokesman became a loose cannon, whose off-script remarks repeatedly undermined the company’s legal strategy.

Cluster IV — Authenticity, Perception, and Generalization

13. Show One’s True Colors

  • Nautical Origin: Ships identified themselves by flying flags known as colors. A vessel might sail under false colors (the flag of a friendly nation) to approach undetected, then haul down the disguise and show its true colors — its real flag — moments before attacking. This was the modus operandi of pirates and privateers.
  • Semantic Core: To reveal one’s genuine character, intentions, or allegiances, typically after a period of concealment. The related idiom to sail under false colors describes sustained deception.
  • Register: Neutral; common in moral and political discourse.
  • Single-Word Equivalent: Reveal oneself; drop the mask; show one’s hand.
  • Polar Opposite: Keep up the facade; sail under false colors.

Model Sentence: The friendly investor showed his true colors when he demanded a controlling stake and the ouster of the founding team.

14. The Cut of One’s Jib

  • Nautical Origin: The jib is a triangular sail set at the bow of a ship. Because different navies and regions cut and shaped their jibs differently, an experienced sailor could often identify a vessel’s nationality — and friend or foe status — from a distance by the cut of its jib.
  • Semantic Core: A person’s outward appearance, style, or demeanor as a basis for a quick (often distrustful) judgment. It most frequently appears in the phrase I don’t like the cut of his jib.
  • Register: Slightly dated and colorful; informal.
  • Single-Word Equivalent: Demeanor, appearance, mien.
  • Polar Opposite: Character (inner, as opposed to outward form).

Model Sentence: The hiring manager admitted he trusted his gut: he didn’t like the cut of the candidate’s jib, and the reference checks confirmed his unease.

15. By and Large

  • Nautical Origin: Sailing by the wind meant steering as close to the wind as possible while still making headway; sailing large meant sailing with the wind well behind the beam. A ship that could sail by and large could handle virtually any wind direction — a mark of a capable vessel.
  • Semantic Core: On the whole; generally; for the most part. It has drifted completely from its nautical senses into a high-frequency discourse marker of generalization.
  • Register: Neutral to formal; standard in academic and analytical prose.
  • Single-Word Equivalent: Generally, overall, in the main.
  • Polar Opposite: Specifically; in particular; strictly.

Model Sentence: The quarterly results were, by and large, encouraging, though the European segment lagged expectations.

Cluster V — Shared Fate, Missed Opportunity, and Excess

16. In the Same Boat

  • Nautical Origin: Passengers and crew aboard a single vessel share its risks; if the ship sinks, all aboard sink with it.
  • Semantic Core: In the same difficult or disadvantageous circumstances as another. It is an idiom of solidarity in adversity.
  • Register: Neutral to informal.
  • Single-Word Equivalent: In the same predicament; in this together.
  • Polar Opposite: In different boats; insulated; unaffected.

Model Sentence: With the whole industry facing new tariffs, competing firms discovered they were all in the same boat.

17. Miss the Boat

  • Nautical Origin: To arrive too late to board a departing vessel — the boat literally sails without you.
  • Semantic Core: To fail to take advantage of an opportunity through delay, hesitation, or inattention. The related that ship has sailed expresses the same idea retrospectively.
  • Register: Neutral to informal.
  • Single-Word Equivalent: Lose the opportunity; miss out.
  • Polar Opposite: Seize the moment; jump aboard; catch the wave.

Model Sentence: Startups that dismissed generative AI in 2023 risk missing the boat on the next platform shift.

18. Three Sheets to the Wind

  • Nautical Origin: A sheet is not a sail but a line (rope) used to trim a sail to the wind. If three sheets are left loose and flapping, the sail loses its set and the ship lurches and wallows erratically — resembling the unsteady stagger of an intoxicated person. The idiom exists on a gradient: two sheets to the wind (merry), three sheets (very drunk), and occasionally four sheets (incapacitated).
  • Semantic Core: Very drunk; staggering, unsteady, and loudly incapable.
  • Register: Informal and humorous; euphemistic rather than clinical.
  • Single-Word Equivalent: Intoxicated, inebriated, blotto.
  • Polar Opposite: Sober; clear-headed; steady as she goes.

Model Sentence: The normally reserved accountant returned from the office party three sheets to the wind, singing sea shanties in the elevator.

Diagnostic Synthesis: Navigating an Integrated Narrative

Observe how the idioms interlock within a single passage describing a corporate crisis:

“When the regulator’s enforcement notice landed, the general counsel barked all hands on deck and told the risk team to batten down the hatches. The compliance lead, who knew the ropes better than anyone, spent the night clearing the decks for an internal review. It was far from smooth sailing: the board feared that a loose cannon in the sales division might show his true colors under questioning. Yet, because the CEO ran a tight ship and kept the organization on an even keel, the firm ultimately weathered the storm — and, by and large, emerged with its reputation intact.”

Knowledge Retention Quiz

1. Which nautical command underlies the idiom “all hands on deck”? A) A request for the captain’s permission to set sail. B) A command summoning every crew member to the deck for urgent work. C) An order to abandon ship. D) A signal that the vessel is about to dock.

2. What is the literal nautical meaning of “batten down the hatches”? A) To raise the sails to full height. B) To seal deck openings with tarpaulin and wooden battens before a storm. C) To lower the anchor. D) To repaint the ship’s hull.

3. What does a “sheet” refer to in the idiom “three sheets to the wind”? A) The canvas sail itself. B) A rope or line used to trim a sail. C) A bunk bed on board. D) A layer of ice on the deck.

4. Why is a “loose cannon” dangerous on a sailing warship? A) It would detonate spontaneously. B) It could break free and roll unpredictably, crushing crew members. C) It would capsize the ship instantly. D) It would sink the ship through its own weight.

5. Which idiom expresses the idea “generally, on the whole”? A) By and large. B) Three sheets to the wind. C) The cut of one’s jib. D) Miss the boat.

6. What is the origin of “show one’s true colors”? A) A ship revealing its real flag before attacking. B) A sailor changing into formal dress. C) A captain lighting signal flares. D) A merchant displaying its cargo.

Explanatory Answer Key

  1. B. The command summoned the entire crew — “all hands” — to the deck for emergency or heavy collective labor.
  2. B. Battens were wooden strips used to fasten tarpaulins over hatches, sealing the ship against flooding.
  3. B. A “sheet” is a line controlling a sail; loose sheets cause the sail to flap and the ship to lurch like a drunk.
  4. B. A cannon broken from its restraints weighed tons and rolled dangerously across the deck.
  5. A. “By and large” derives from a ship’s ability to sail both “by the wind” and “large,” and now means “generally.”
  6. A. A vessel might sail under false colors, then reveal its genuine flag — its “true colors” — before striking.

* **Semantic Core:** To disturb a stable situation by raising objections, making controversial changes, or challenging the status quo. It is often used in the negative (*don't rock the boat*).
* **Register:** Neutral to informal.
* **Single-Word Equivalent:** *Disturb the status quo; make waves; cause trouble.*
* **Polar Opposite:** *Keep the peace; go along; steady the ship.*

> **Model Sentence:** She decided not to **rock the boat** during her first month, quietly observing the team's dynamics before proposing reform.

### 11. Smooth Sailing (Plain Sailing)

* **Nautical Origin:** **Plain sailing** (originally *plane sailing*) referred to a simplified navigational method that treated a short stretch of ocean as a flat plane, making course calculations easy. The spelling **plain** later won out, reinforcing the sense of *simple, unobstructed*.
* **Semantic Core:** Easy, trouble-free progress toward a goal. It frequently appears with a caveat — *it's not all smooth sailing*.
* **Register:** Neutral to informal.
* **Single-Word Equivalent:** *Effortless, straightforward, unimpeded.*
* **Polar Opposite:** *Heavy going; rough seas; an uphill battle.*

> **Model Sentence:** The migration to the new database was surprisingly **smooth sailing**, thanks to months of dry-run rehearsal.

### 12. Loose Cannon

* **Nautical Origin:** On wooden warships, cannon weighed several tons and were lashed to the gunports. In battle or heavy seas, a cannon could break free of its restraints and roll unpredictably across the deck, crushing anything — and anyone — in its path.
* **Semantic Core:** A person or situation that is dangerously unpredictable, reckless, and beyond control. It describes a **liability to one's own side** as much as a threat to others.
* **Register:** Neutral; widely used in politics, media, and management.
* **Single-Word Equivalent:** *Wildcard; loose wire; uncontrollable element.*
* **Polar Opposite:** *Steady hand; team player; reliable operator.*

> **Model Sentence:** The charismatic but erratic spokesman became a **loose cannon**, whose off-script remarks repeatedly undermined the company's legal strategy.

## Cluster IV — Authenticity, Perception, and Generalization

### 13. Show One's True Colors

* **Nautical Origin:** Ships identified themselves by flying flags known as **colors**. A vessel might sail under **false colors** (the flag of a friendly nation) to approach undetected, then haul down the disguise and **show its true colors** — its real flag — moments before attacking. This was the modus operandi of pirates and privateers.
* **Semantic Core:** To reveal one's genuine character, intentions, or allegiances, typically after a period of concealment. The related idiom *to sail under false colors* describes sustained deception.
* **Register:** Neutral; common in moral and political discourse.
* **Single-Word Equivalent:** *Reveal oneself; drop the mask; show one's hand.*
* **Polar Opposite:** *Keep up the facade; sail under false colors.*

> **Model Sentence:** The friendly investor **showed his true colors** when he demanded a controlling stake and the ouster of the founding team.

### 14. The Cut of One's Jib

* **Nautical Origin:** The **jib** is a triangular sail set at the bow of a ship. Because different navies and regions cut and shaped their jibs differently, an experienced sailor could often identify a vessel's nationality — and friend or foe status — from a distance by **the cut of its jib**.
* **Semantic Core:** A person's outward appearance, style, or demeanor as a basis for a quick (often distrustful) judgment. It most frequently appears in the phrase *I don't like the cut of his jib*.
* **Register:** Slightly dated and colorful; informal.
* **Single-Word Equivalent:** *Demeanor, appearance, mien.*
* **Polar Opposite:** *Character (inner, as opposed to outward form).*

> **Model Sentence:** The hiring manager admitted he trusted his gut: he didn't like **the cut of the candidate's jib**, and the reference checks confirmed his unease.

### 15. By and Large

* **Nautical Origin:** Sailing **by the wind** meant steering as close to the wind as possible while still making headway; sailing **large** meant sailing with the wind well behind the beam. A ship that could sail *by and large* could handle virtually any wind direction — a mark of a capable vessel.
* **Semantic Core:** **On the whole; generally; for the most part.** It has drifted completely from its nautical senses into a high-frequency discourse marker of generalization.
* **Register:** Neutral to formal; standard in academic and analytical prose.
* **Single-Word Equivalent:** *Generally, overall, in the main.*
* **Polar Opposite:** *Specifically; in particular; strictly.*

> **Model Sentence:** The quarterly results were, **by and large**, encouraging, though the European segment lagged expectations.

## Cluster V — Shared Fate, Missed Opportunity, and Excess

### 16. In the Same Boat

* **Nautical Origin:** Passengers and crew aboard a single vessel share its risks; if the ship sinks, all aboard sink with it.
* **Semantic Core:** In the same difficult or disadvantageous circumstances as another. It is an idiom of **solidarity in adversity**.
* **Register:** Neutral to informal.
* **Single-Word Equivalent:** *In the same predicament; in this together.*
* **Polar Opposite:** *In different boats; insulated; unaffected.*

> **Model Sentence:** With the whole industry facing new tariffs, competing firms discovered they were all **in the same boat**.

### 17. Miss the Boat

* **Nautical Origin:** To arrive too late to board a departing vessel — the boat literally sails without you.
* **Semantic Core:** To fail to take advantage of an opportunity through delay, hesitation, or inattention. The related *that ship has sailed* expresses the same idea retrospectively.
* **Register:** Neutral to informal.
* **Single-Word Equivalent:** *Lose the opportunity; miss out.*
* **Polar Opposite:** *Seize the moment; jump aboard; catch the wave.*

> **Model Sentence:** Startups that dismissed generative AI in 2023 risk **missing the boat** on the next platform shift.

### 18. Three Sheets to the Wind

* **Nautical Origin:** A **sheet** is not a sail but a line (rope) used to trim a sail to the wind. If three sheets are left loose and flapping, the sail loses its set and the ship lurches and wallows erratically — resembling the unsteady stagger of an intoxicated person. The idiom exists on a gradient: *two sheets to the wind* (merry), *three sheets* (very drunk), and occasionally *four sheets* (incapacitated).
* **Semantic Core:** Very drunk; staggering, unsteady, and loudly incapable.
* **Register:** Informal and humorous; euphemistic rather than clinical.
* **Single-Word Equivalent:** *Intoxicated, inebriated, blotto.*
* **Polar Opposite:** *Sober; clear-headed; steady as she goes.*

> **Model Sentence:** The normally reserved accountant returned from the office party **three sheets to the wind**, singing sea shanties in the elevator.

## Diagnostic Synthesis: Navigating an Integrated Narrative

Observe how the idioms interlock within a single passage describing a corporate crisis:

> "When the regulator's enforcement notice landed, the general counsel barked **all hands on deck** and told the risk team to **batten down the hatches**. The compliance lead, who **knew the ropes** better than anyone, spent the night **clearing the decks** for an internal review. It was far from **smooth sailing**: the board feared that a **loose cannon** in the sales division might **show his true colors** under questioning. Yet, because the CEO **ran a tight ship** and kept the organization **on an even keel**, the firm ultimately **weathered the storm** — and, **by and large**, emerged with its reputation intact."

## Knowledge Retention Quiz

**1. Which nautical command underlies the idiom "all hands on deck"?**
A) A request for the captain's permission to set sail.
B) A command summoning every crew member to the deck for urgent work.
C) An order to abandon ship.
D) A signal that the vessel is about to dock.

**2. What is the literal nautical meaning of "batten down the hatches"?**
A) To raise the sails to full height.
B) To seal deck openings with tarpaulin and wooden battens before a storm.
C) To lower the anchor.
D) To repaint the ship's hull.

**3. What does a "sheet" refer to in the idiom "three sheets to the wind"?**
A) The canvas sail itself.
B) A rope or line used to trim a sail.
C) A bunk bed on board.
D) A layer of ice on the deck.

**4. Why is a "loose cannon" dangerous on a sailing warship?**
A) It would detonate spontaneously.
B) It could break free and roll unpredictably, crushing crew members.
C) It would capsize the ship instantly.
D) It would sink the ship through its own weight.

**5. Which idiom expresses the idea "generally, on the whole"?**
A) By and large.
B) Three sheets to the wind.
C) The cut of one's jib.
D) Miss the boat.

**6. What is the origin of "show one's true colors"?**
A) A ship revealing its real flag before attacking.
B) A sailor changing into formal dress.
C) A captain lighting signal flares.
D) A merchant displaying its cargo.

### Explanatory Answer Key
1. **B.** The command summoned the entire crew — "all hands" — to the deck for emergency or heavy collective labor.
2. **B.** Battens were wooden strips used to fasten tarpaulins over hatches, sealing the ship against flooding.
3. **B.** A "sheet" is a line controlling a sail; loose sheets cause the sail to flap and the ship to lurch like a drunk.
4. **B.** A cannon broken from its restraints weighed tons and rolled dangerously across the deck.
5. **A.** "By and large" derives from a ship's ability to sail both "by the wind" and "large," and now means "generally."
6. **A.** A vessel might sail under false colors, then reveal its genuine flag — its "true colors" — before striking.
