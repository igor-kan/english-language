Introduction: Saying “It Happened”

To say that something happened is one of the most basic functions of language — yet English offers an extraordinary range of expressions for it, each carrying its own register, emotional colour, and theological or philosophical overtone. At the majestic, archaic end sits come to pass; at the casual end, pan out and go down. Between them lie dozens of nuanced options.

This article maps the whole family, with particular attention to the celebrated “transpire” controversy — one of English’s most instructive usage disputes.

1. Come to Pass

Phonetics: /kʌm tə pɑːs/.

Meaning

To happen; to occur; especially, to be fulfilled (of a prophecy, prediction, or expectation). The phrase carries a strong implication of destiny or foretelling: what “comes to pass” was, in some sense, expected to come.

  • “All that he had foretold came to pass.”
  • “It came to pass that the two rivals met again at the summit.”
  • “Her worst fears came to pass.”

Etymological and Biblical Architecture

The phrase is a Hebraism — a construction imported from Biblical Hebrew through Greek and Latin. The Hebrew narrative formula wayehi (“and it happened / and it came about”) was rendered in the Greek Septuagint as kai egeneto and in the Latin Vulgate as factum est. The King James Bible (1611) translated it as “and it came to pass” — a formula that recurs roughly 450 times in the KJV. This scriptural saturation is precisely why the phrase sounds so solemn today.

Here, pass is used in its older sense of “to happen, occur” — a sense preserved in happen’s archaic cousin hap (as in perhaps, mishap, haphazard).

Register and Usage

  • Register: archaic, biblical, literary, ceremonial. It is rare in ordinary speech.
  • Effect in modern use: dignity, gravity, or — deliberately — irony and mock-grandeur.
  • Grammar: typically impersonal with it (“it came to pass that…”), or takes a plural subject (“these things came to pass”).
  • Related archaic form: bring to pass = to cause to happen (“He brought his promise to pass”).

Model Sentence: “For years the analysts had warned of a liquidity crisis — and in the autumn of that year, it came to pass.”

2. Come About

Meaning: To happen, especially through a process, a chain of events, or by chance; frequently used of change.

  • “How did this situation come about?”
  • “The reform came about after decades of campaigning.”

Nuance: Unlike come to pass (which stresses fulfilment), come about stresses causation and process. It is common in British English and neutral-to-formal.

Separate nautical sense: Of a ship, to come about is to change tack (turn through the wind).

3. Come to Be / Come into Being

  • Come to be: to begin to exist or arise — “No one knows how the custom came to be.”
  • Come into being / existence: to be created or brought into existence — “A new nation came into being in 1947.”

4. Come True

Of a wish, dream, hope, or prediction: to be realized. * “Her lifelong ambition finally came true.” * “Be careful what you wish for — it might come true.”

5. Transpire — The Great Usage Controversy

Phonetics: /trænˈspaɪər/.

Etymological Architecture

From Latin trans- (“through, across”) + spirare (“to breathe”) — literally “to breathe through,” hence “to evaporate, escape as vapour.” The word travelled from the physical (moisture transpiring through a surface) to the informational.

The Two Competing Senses

A. The Traditional Sense: To Become Known, to Leak Out

“Transpire” originally came to mean, of a secret or fact, to become known or to come to light: * “It later transpired that the minister had known all along.” * “Nothing transpired about the closed-door meeting.”

B. The Disputed Sense: To Happen, to Occur

In the 18th century, a second sense emerged — transpire as a synonym for happen: * “What transpired at the meeting remains unclear.”

Why It Is Controversial

Traditional authorities (and much British usage) object that this second sense is a misuse, since the word’s proper function is to describe information becoming known, not events occurring. American English, however, has adopted the “happen” sense widely, and many modern dictionaries now list it as fully standard in that register.

Practical guidance: In careful formal prose, reserve transpire for “become known” (“it transpired that…”) and use happen, occur, take place, or come about for events. Avoid “what transpired” if your audience is likely to be prescriptive.

6. Befall and Betide

Befall

Meaning: (of something, usually unwelcome) to happen to a person. Literary and formal. * “A terrible tragedy befell the family.” * “Whatever befalls us, we face it together.”

Etymology: Old English befallan = be- (“about”) + feallan (“to fall”) — literally “to fall to (someone’s lot).”

Exception/restriction: Befall almost always takes a negative or fateful event as its subject (misfortune, disaster, tragedy), and the person affected is the object.

Betide

Meaning: To happen to; to befall. Now archaic/literary, fossilized chiefly in the threat “woe betide”: * “Woe betide anyone who interrupts him.” (= misfortune will come to them) * “Whatever betide, we shall endure.”

Etymology: Old English betīdan = be- + tīdan (“to happen”), from the root of tide and time.

7. Materialize, Pan Out, Unfold, Eventuate, Come Off

Expression Meaning Register Nuance
Materialize To become actual or real Neutral Often implies doubt: “the funding never materialized.”
Pan out To turn out; to succeed Informal “It didn’t pan out.”
Unfold To develop or be revealed gradually Neutral/literary “Events unfolded rapidly.”
Eventuate To result (in) Very formal, chiefly BrE “The dispute eventuated in war.”
Come off To happen; to succeed Informal (BrE) “The wedding came off beautifully.”
Go down To happen Informal “What went down last night?”
Come to be To arise, begin to exist Neutral/formal “How did this come to be?”

Note on come off: It has several other senses: to succeed (“the trick came off”), to leave (“come off the stage”), and in the exclamation “Come off it!” (= stop talking nonsense).

9. Cases and Exceptions to Watch

  • “Come to pass” is virtually never used in casual speech. Deploy it only for deliberate gravity or irony.
  • The “transpire” trap. Traditionalists reject transpire = happen. Know your audience.
  • “Befall” takes bad news. You rarely say “good fortune befell him” — the word leans ominous.
  • “Betide” survives mainly in threats: “woe betide…”.
  • “Pan out” is informal and often dismissive of grand plans.
  • The hap family: perhaps, mishap, haphazard, haply are fossilized cousins of “happen.”
  • Register mismatch is the classic error: “It transpired that…” next to “things panned out” creates tonal whiplash.

10. Diagnostic Synthesis

“The prophecy, long dismissed as superstition, came to pass on a grey October morning. It transpired that the two signatories had known each other for years, though how their alliance came about no one could say. As events unfolded, the promised reforms simply never materialized; the grand plan did not pan out, and a quiet dread befell the city.”
