Introduction: The Grammar of Before and After

Time is one of the deepest organizing dimensions of human language. English encodes temporal order not with a single family of words but with several overlapping systems — some Latinate and formal (precede, antecedent, subsequent), some Germanic and plain (before, after, follow), and some deceptively similar (predate vs. antedate; forego vs. forgo). This article maps the vocabulary of precedence and succession with precision.

[ THE TEMPORAL AXIS ]

   EARLIER ◄────────────────────────────────────────► LATER

   predate      precede      follow       postdate
   antedate     come before  come after   succeed
   antecedent   precursor    sequel       successor
   forerunner   harbinger    consequence  outcome

1. Predate

Phonetics: /priːˈdeɪt/.

Meaning: To exist or occur before something else in time; to be earlier than.

  • “Dinosaurs predate humans by hundreds of millions of years.”
  • “The custom predates the founding of the city.”
  • “This manuscript predates the printed edition.”

Etymology: pre- (“before”) + date. Note that predate does not mean “to date a document earlier”; that is antedate.

Register: Neutral to formal; the standard verb for “X is older than Y.”

2. Antedate

Phonetics: /ˌæntiˈdeɪt/.

Two senses: 1. To precede in time (a near-synonym of predate): “The invention of paper antedates the printing press.” 2. To assign an earlier date to a document or event than the true one: “The letter was antedated to appear older.” (The opposite of postdate.)

Etymology: Latin ante- (“before”) + date.

Nuance: Antedate is rarer and more formal than predate. Its second, documentary sense (falsely dating earlier) is unique to it and postdate.

3. Precede

Phonetics: /prɪˈsiːd/.

Meaning: To come before in time, order, rank, or position.

  • “A short silence preceded the applause.”
  • “The chairman is preceded by his deputy in the procession.”

Etymology: Latin praecedere = prae- (“before”) + cedere (“to go”). Literally, “to go before.”

Direct object required: Precede is transitive. You do not “precede to” something.

Model Sentence: “The briefing preceded the vote by three hours, allowing the members to weigh the evidence.”

4. Postdate

Phonetics: /ˌpəʊstˈdeɪt/.

Two senses: 1. To come after in time: “The painting postdates the artist’s Italian period.” 2. To assign a later date to a document than the true one: “He postdated the cheque to the first of the month.”

Antonym: antedate (in the documentary sense); predate / antedate (in the chronological sense).

5. Succeed and Follow

Succeed

Meaning: To come after someone or something (in time, order, or office). This is the direct antonym of precede. * “George VI succeeded Edward VIII to the throne.” (Note the preposition to.) * “A period of calm succeeded the storm.”

Homonym trap: Succeed also means “to achieve success” (“she succeeded in business”). Context disambiguates: succeed to a position/office = inherit it.

Follow

Meaning: To come after — the plain, versatile Germanic antonym of precede. * “Night follows day.” * “The error followed a long silence.” * Also figurative: “It follows that the premise is false.”

6. The Forego / Forgo Trap

This is one of the most frequently confused pairs in English:

Word Meaning Example
Forego (fore + go) To precede, go before (archaic/rare) “A fanfare foregoes the procession.”
Forgo (for + go) To do without, abstain from “She forwent dessert.”

Because forego is now rare, modern writers overwhelmingly use forgo — and often misspell it as “forego” when they mean “do without.” If you mean “abstain,” write forgo.

7. The Precedence Constellation: Nouns

Noun Meaning Example
Antecedent A thing that existed or occurred before; (grammar) the word a pronoun replaces “Deregulation was the antecedent of the crisis.”
Predecessor One who came before in a role or office “Her predecessor left a large deficit.”
Precursor Something that comes before and indicates or leads to another “The riots were a precursor to revolution.”
Forerunner A person or thing that precedes and signals another’s arrival “The Model T was the forerunner of mass motoring.”
Harbinger A sign of what is to come (often ominous) “Swallows are a harbinger of spring.”
Prelude An introductory action or event preceding something greater “The skirmish was a prelude to war.”
Omen / portent A prophetic sign “A portent of disaster.”
Prototype An early model from which later versions derive “The prototype preceded the production car.”

Nuance Notes

  • Precursor is technical/neutral; forerunner is more general; harbinger is literary and usually signals something momentous or threatening.
  • Antecedent (in grammar) names the noun that a pronoun refers to — a distinct technical sense worth knowing.
  • Predecessor always implies a succession of persons in a role; ancestor implies lineage.

8. The Succession Constellation: Nouns

Noun Meaning Example
Successor One who comes after in a role “His successor reversed the policy.”
Sequel A work or event that follows another “The treaty was the sequel to the war.”
Consequence A result that follows from a cause “A consequence of the delay.”
Aftermath The period following a significant (often destructive) event “In the aftermath of the flood.”
Epilogue A concluding section “The epilogue wraps up the tale.”
Descendant One who comes from an ancestor “A descendant of the original settlers.”

9. Simultaneity and Overlap

When events are not in sequence but coincide:

Word Meaning Example
Simultaneous At exactly the same time “Simultaneous translation.”
Concurrent Happening at the same time; overlapping “Concurrent sentences.”
Contemporaneous Existing or occurring at the same period “Contemporaneous accounts of the battle.”
Coeval Of the same age or era (formal) “Coeval with the pyramids.”
Synchronous Occurring at the same time/rate (technical) “Synchronous communication.”
Contemporary Living/existing at the same time; modern “A contemporary of Shakespeare.”

Nuance: Simultaneous stresses exact coincidence; concurrent stresses overlap; contemporaneous stresses the same historical period; synchronous is the technical term (systems, signals).

10. Adjectives of Order

Earlier Later
prior, previous, former, erstwhile, antecedent, preliminary subsequent, following, ensuing, consequent, later, subsequent to
anterior (technical) posterior (technical)
ex- (former): ex-president incoming, designate (president-elect)

Retroactive / retroactively: applying to a period before enactment — “a retroactive pay rise.”

11. Model Sentences

“The brief calm that preceded the storm was a harbinger of what followed. The events that succeeded it had been antedated in rumor for years — a prelude, perhaps, to conflicts whose aftermath would outlast its architects.”

12. Knowledge Retention Quiz

1. Which verb means to exist before something else in time? A) postdate B) predate C) succeed D) follow

2. Which word means “to do without” (not “to precede”)? A) forego B) forgo C) antedate D) postdate

3. What is the direct antonym of “precede”? A) predate B) succeed C) repeat D) antedate

4. Which noun means “a sign of what is to come, often ominous”? A) sequel B) successor C) harbinger D) consequence

5. Which pair describes events occurring at the same historical period? A) coeval / contemporaneous B) prior / posterior C) antecedent / sequent D) prelude / epilogue

Answer Key & Rationale

  1. B. Predate means to be earlier than.
  2. B. Forgo means to abstain from; forego (rare) means to precede.
  3. B. Succeed means to come after — the antonym of precede.
  4. C. A harbinger is a forerunner signalling what is to come.
  5. A. Coeval and contemporaneous mean existing in the same period.

# Introduction: The Grammar of Before and After

Time is one of the deepest organizing dimensions of human language. English encodes temporal order not with a single family of words but with several overlapping systems — some Latinate and formal (*precede, antecedent, subsequent*), some Germanic and plain (*before, after, follow*), and some deceptively similar (*predate* vs. *antedate*; *forego* vs. *forgo*). ## 1. Predate

**Phonetics:** /priːˈdeɪt/.

**Meaning:** To exist or occur **before** something else in time; to be earlier than.

* *"Dinosaurs **predate** humans by hundreds of millions of years."*
* *"The custom **predates** the founding of the city."*
* *"This manuscript **predates** the printed edition."*

**Etymology:** *pre-* ("before") + *date*. Note that *predate* does **not** mean "to date a document earlier"; that is *antedate*.

**Register:** Neutral to formal; the standard verb for "X is older than Y."

---

## 2. Antedate

**Phonetics:** /ˌæntiˈdeɪt/.

**Two senses:**
1. **To precede in time** (a near-synonym of *predate*): *"The invention of paper **antedates** the printing press."*
2. **To assign an earlier date to a document or event than the true one:** *"The letter was **antedated** to appear older."* (The opposite of *postdate*.)

**Etymology:** Latin *ante-* ("before") + *date*.

**Nuance:** *Antedate* is rarer and more formal than *predate*. Its second, documentary sense (falsely dating earlier) is unique to it and *postdate*.

---

## 3. Precede

**Phonetics:** /prɪˈsiːd/.

**Meaning:** To come **before** in time, order, rank, or position.

* *"A short silence **preceded** the applause."*
* *"The chairman is **preceded** by his deputy in the procession."*

**Etymology:** Latin *praecedere* = *prae-* ("before") + *cedere* ("to go"). Literally, *"to go before."*

**Direct object required:** *Precede* is transitive. You do not "precede to" something.

> **Model Sentence:** *"The briefing **preceded** the vote by three hours, allowing the members to weigh the evidence."*

---

## 4. Postdate

**Phonetics:** /ˌpəʊstˈdeɪt/.

**Two senses:**
1. **To come after in time:** *"The painting **postdates** the artist's Italian period."*
2. **To assign a later date to a document than the true one:** *"He **postdated** the cheque to the first of the month."*

**Antonym:** *antedate* (in the documentary sense); *predate* / *antedate* (in the chronological sense).

---

## 5. Succeed and Follow

### Succeed
**Meaning:** To come **after** someone or something (in time, order, or office). This is the direct antonym of *precede*.
* *"George VI **succeeded** Edward VIII to the throne."* (Note the preposition *to*.)
* *"A period of calm **succeeded** the storm."*

> **Homonym trap:** *Succeed* also means "to achieve success" (*"she succeeded in business"*). Context disambiguates: *succeed to* a position/office = inherit it.

### Follow
**Meaning:** To come after — the plain, versatile Germanic antonym of *precede*.
* *"Night **follows** day."*
* *"The error **followed** a long silence."*
* Also figurative: *"It **follows** that the premise is false."*

## 6. The Forego / Forgo Trap

This is one of the most frequently confused pairs in English:

| Word | Meaning | Example |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Forego** (*fore* + *go*) | To **precede**, go before (archaic/rare) | *"A fanfare **foregoes** the procession."* |
| **Forgo** (*for* + *go*) | To **do without**, abstain from | *"She **forwent** dessert."* |

Because *forego* is now rare, modern writers overwhelmingly use *forgo* — and often misspell it as "forego" when they mean "do without." **If you mean "abstain," write *forgo*.**
