Introduction: The Metaphor of “Apart”

The adverb and particle apart means “into pieces” or “separated by distance or distinction.” Attached to verbs of placing, taking, coming, and tearing, it generates two broad conceptual families:

  1. Distinction and separation — marking something as different, or keeping things at a distance (set apart, tell apart, stand apart, keep apart, drift apart).
  2. Disintegration and disassembly — breaking something into its parts, literally or argumentatively (come apart, fall apart, take apart, tear apart, pick apart).

Like the aside family, these verbs are near-synonyms with sharply different force: set apart elevates, take apart dismantles, tear apart destroys. Choosing among them is a matter of register, transitivity, and emotional temperature.

[ THE APART SPECTRUM ]

  DISTINCTION ──► set apart / tell apart ──► stand apart ──► a class apart
  (elevate, differentiate)                  (be conspicuous)   (idiomatic)

  SEPARATION ──► keep apart / hold apart ──► drift apart / grow apart
  (maintain distance)                       (lose closeness)

  DISINTEGRATION ──► come apart / fall apart ──► break apart / split apart
  (intransitive)                                (come to pieces)

  DISASSEMBLY ──► take apart / pull apart ──► pick apart / tear apart
  (transitive)                              (criticize, destroy)

1. Set Apart

The flagship member of the family, and the one most often required in formal prose. It has three distinct senses.

A. To Distinguish or Elevate

To mark someone or something as fundamentally different — usually superior — to others of the same class:

  • “What sets elite translators apart is not vocabulary size but ear for register.”

  • “Her clarity of thought set her apart from her contemporaries.”

  • “A capacity for delayed gratification is what sets the species apart.”

  • Equivalents: distinguish, differentiate, elevate, single out, mark out.

  • Polar opposites: assimilate, homogenize, blend in, merge with the crowd.

Particle placement: in this sense the object normally sits between set and apart — set someone/something apart (from). “Set apart the best students” is possible but far less idiomatic than “set the best students apart.”

B. To Reserve or Consecrate

To dedicate something to a special purpose, often with a sacred or solemn colouring:

  • “The land was set apart for a nature reserve.”

  • “He was set apart for the priesthood.”

  • “These hours are set apart for deep work.”

  • Equivalents: consecrate, dedicate, earmark, reserve, hallow.

Nuance: this sense descends directly from the biblical and liturgical language of being “set apart” — chosen, sanctified, withdrawn from ordinary use. It explains why the phrase can sound either ceremonial or, in secular usage, merely administrative (“funds set apart for repairs”).

C. Set Apart From — The Comparative Frame

The construction X sets Y apart from Z is the workhorse of evaluative writing:

  • “What sets this algorithm apart from its rivals is its worst-case guarantee.”

Note that the compared class is introduced by from, never than: set apart than is a common error.

2. Tell Apart (and Know Apart)

To distinguish two or more similar things by perceiving their differences:

  • “I can’t tell the twins apart.”
  • “Only an expert can tell the two vintages apart.”
  • “You can tell them apart by the stitching.”

Usage Notes

  • Tell apart is overwhelmingly used in the negative or with a modal (can/can’t, could/couldn’t, hard to, difficult to). Plain affirmative “I tell them apart” sounds unnatural outside a contrast (“I can tell them apart, but my brother can’t”).
  • The object must be plural or a pair: tell the twins apart, tell them apart — not “tell the twin apart.”
  • A rarer, more formal variant is know apart: “The counterfeit is difficult to know apart from the original.”

Compare: tell apart concerns perception (can I see the difference?); set apart concerns status (what makes it superior?). You tell apart similar things; you set apart exceptional ones.

3. Come Apart and Fall Apart

Both are intransitive — the subject disintegrates by itself — but their rhythm differs.

Come Apart

To separate into pieces, often at a seam, joint, or weak point; also, of a plan or person, to break down:

  • “The old binding came apart in my hands.”
  • “The coalition came apart over the budget.”
  • “Under cross-examination, his story came apart.”

Fall Apart

To break into pieces and collapse; figuratively, to fail completely or to suffer an emotional breakdown:

  • “The book fell apart after one reading.”
  • “Their marriage fell apart.”
  • “The whole plan fell apart at the last minute.”
  • “After the diagnosis, she fell apart.”

Fixed Expressions

Expression Meaning
Fall apart at the seams Fail at every weak point simultaneously
Things fall apart An orderly system collapses (echoing Yeats and Achebe)
Come apart at the seams Approach total collapse

Nuance: come apart suggests separation into components (a mechanism, a case, an argument); fall apart suggests collapse into ruin (a structure, a relationship, a life). Fall apart is the stronger, more emotive choice.

4. Take Apart

To disassemble something into its constituents, either physically or — very commonly — argumentatively:

  • “He took the engine apart and rebuilt it.”

  • “The reviewer took the paper apart, paragraph by paragraph.”

  • “She took apart every claim in the manifesto.”

  • Equivalents: dismantle, disassemble, dissect, deconstruct, demolish (figurative).

  • Polar opposites: assemble, put together, reassemble, construct.

Nuance: take apart in the critical sense is methodical, not merely hostile — the dismantling proceeds piece by piece. It is the natural verb of rigorous critique and of deconstruction.

Related: the noun a takedown (a devastating critical attack) and the adjective take-apart in technical writing (“a take-apart teardown of the console”).

5. Pull Apart, Tear Apart, Rip Apart

A trio of forceful, transitive verbs with overlapping but distinct imagery.

Verb Core image Typical objects
Pull apart Drawing two joined things away from each other combatants, factions, a couple, tissue
Tear apart Rending with violence; savaging a family, a reputation, an argument, a book
Rip apart Violent, abrupt rending clothing, a contract, an opponent’s case
  • “Security pulled the two delegates apart.”
  • “The scandal tore the community apart.”
  • “Critics tore apart the film on release.”
  • “She ripped apart the proposal in open session.”

Nuance: pull apart is the least violent and most physical; it implies separating things that are gripped together, and it is the standard verb for intervening in a fight. Tear apart and rip apart belong to emotional and rhetorical destruction — a family torn apart, a thesis ripped apart. Rip apart is the most informal and the most aggressive.

6. Pick Apart

To examine minutely in order to find faults — the verb of hostile close reading:

  • “The auditors picked apart every line of the accounts.”

  • “He picked apart my reasoning until nothing was left.”

  • “Stop picking apart every word I say.”

  • Equivalents: nitpick, fault-find, scrutinize, cavil at, quibble over.

Same idiom, different phrasing: pick holes in and pick to pieces mean almost the same thing, as does the verb fault (“I can’t fault the logic”). Pick apart implies systematic, item-by-item criticism rather than one sweeping verdict — compare tear apart, which delivers the verdict by force.

7. Break Apart, Split Apart, Blow Apart, Burst Apart

The violent-separation set.

Verb Sense Example
Break apart Come to pieces; force into pieces “The ice shelf broke apart.”
Split apart Divide along a line, especially in groups “The party split apart over the vote.”
Blow apart Destroy by explosion (often figurative) “The new evidence blew apart the prosecution’s case.”
Burst apart Rupture from internal pressure “The pipe burst apart in the frost.”
Sunder (single verb) Poetic/literary “split apart” “a family sundered by war”

Nuance: split apart is the natural choice for factions, groups, and organizations; blow apart is the metaphorical explosion of a theory, alibi, or record; break apart is the neutral physical default.

8. Keeping Things Apart: Keep, Hold, Stand, Live, Drift, Grow

A subfamily about maintaining or losing separation.

A. Keep Apart / Hold Apart / Keep (Them) Apart

  • “Keep the wet specimens apart from the dry ones.”
  • “The referee held the players apart.”
  • “It is wise to keep work and friendship apart.”

B. Stand Apart — To Be Conspicuously Different

  • “One candidate stands apart from the rest in experience.”
  • Equivalents: stand out, be in a class of one, tower above.

Nuance: stand apart is approving and evaluative — it praises distinction. Stand off (withhold; keep at a distance) and stand aloof (be emotionally detached) are related but carry coldness, not admiration.

C. Live Apart / Sleep Apart / Sit Apart — Physical Separation

  • “They have lived apart for a year.”
  • “The guests were seated apart from the family.”
  • Equivalents (relationship): separated, estranged (“a legally separated couple”).

D. Drift Apart / Grow Apart — The Slow Estrangement

These two are near-synonyms and are frequently used of friends, couples, and allies:

Verb Emphasis
Drift apart Separation by gradual, passive movement — no one decides it
Grow apart Separation through divergent development over time
  • “We drifted apart after university.”
  • “The two industries have grown apart in culture and method.”

Nuance: drift implies loss of momentum (like boats on a current); grow implies divergence through development (like branches). Neither implies hostility — that is what distinguishes them from fall out (a quarrel) and break up (an ending). To emphasise the intentional element there is the figurative pull in different directions and the verb diverge.

9. Apart From — Exception and Addition

The prepositional construction apart from has two opposite readings, and context alone disambiguates them.

A. Except For (= with the exclusion of)

  • “Apart from the finale, the concert was flawless.”
  • “Apart from a minor injury, the squad is fit.”

B. In Addition To (= as well as)

  • “Apart from teaching, she runs a small press.”
  • “Apart from the cost, there is the question of time.”

The classic ambiguity: “Apart from the noise, the hotel was perfect” can mean (a) the noise was the sole defect, or (b) the noise was one flaw among others. When precision matters, use except for for (a) and besides / as well as for (b). Apart from is a British-favoured form; American English more often writes aside from.

C. “Quite Apart From” — The Rhetorical Escalator

An emphatic frame that adds a second, weightier consideration: “Quite apart from the expense, the plan is illegal.” Here quite apart from means roughly “even leaving aside” — compare the discourse frame “setting aside X…” in the aside family.

  • Equivalents: quite apart from, setting aside, leaving aside, aside from, independent of, not to mention.

10. Adverbial “Apart”: Worlds Apart, Poles Apart, A Class Apart

Away from each other — used in a set of idiomatic complements that denote extreme distance or distinction.

Expression Meaning
Worlds apart Utterly different in outlook or quality
Poles apart At opposite extremes (from the two poles)
Miles apart Very far apart, literally or in agreement
A class apart Superior to all others in its category
A cut above Related idiom: distinctly better than
Two sides of the same coin The reverse idiom: seemingly opposite, fundamentally linked
Joking apart A discourse marker: “seriously now”
Kidding aside / joking aside The aside-family equivalents
  • “Their worldviews are worlds apart.”
  • “On this question the two economists are poles apart.”
  • “The finished instrument is a class apart.”
  • “Joking apart, we need a decision by Friday.”

Note: worlds apart and poles apart are predicative only — they follow be or stand, they do not precede a noun. You cannot say “a worlds-apart pair.”

12. Cases and Exceptions

  • Particle placement (separable vs. inseparable). Most of these are separable when the object is a noun: set the twins apart, take the engine apart, tear the family apart. With a pronoun the particle must follow: set them apart, take it apart — never “take apart it.”
  • The “a part” vs. “apart” trap. Apart (one word) = separated; a part (two words) = a piece of a whole. So: “She is a part of the team” (a member) versus “She stands apart from the team” (separate). The errors “apart of the team” and “take a part the engine” both collapse this distinction. Compare the parallel trap in alot / a lot.
  • “Tell apart” needs a negative or modal. “I can tell them apart” is acceptable as an assertion or contrast; “I tell them apart” is not idiomatic.
  • “Set apart” takes from, never than. “What sets it apart from the rest” — not “than the rest.”
  • “Apart from” is ambiguous. Use except for (exclusion) or besides (addition) when the reading matters.
  • “Apart” is predicative in idioms. Worlds apart, poles apart, a class apart do not precede nouns.
  • “Fall apart” ≠ “fall out.” Fall apart is disintegration; fall out is a quarrel (“they fell out over money”) — entirely different, and a common learner confusion.
  • “Take apart” is not “take part.” Take part in means to participate (“take part in the debate”); take apart means to disassemble. The presence or absence of a space between a and part changes the meaning completely.
  • “Stand apart” and “set apart” are not interchangeable. Set apart is usually done by something (“her wit sets her apart”); stand apart is a state (“she stands apart from her peers”).

13. Diagnostic Synthesis

“What set the young engineer apart was her refusal to accept a result she could not explain. When a rival’s dataset didn’t add up, she took the pipeline apart, picked apart every transformation, and tore apart the claim in the review meeting — yet she never told apart a genuine discovery from a lucky accident without testing it herself. Meanwhile, the team she had left drifted apart; what had once been worlds apart in method were now a class apart in reputation.”

14. Knowledge Retention Quiz

1. Which verb is used almost exclusively in the negative or with a modal, and means “perceive the difference between”? A) set apart B) tell apart C) take apart D) stand apart

2. In “set apart,” where does a noun object normally sit? A) After “apart” — set apart the students B) Between set and apart — set the students apart C) Before set — the students set apart D) It cannot take a noun object

3. Which pair correctly matches the nuance? A) drift apart = a deliberate decision; grow apart = a quarrel B) drift apart = passive, gradual separation; grow apart = separation through divergent development C) drift apart = a physical fight; grow apart = moving away D) They are exact synonyms in every context

4. What is the difference between “come apart” and “fall apart”? A) “Come apart” implies collapse into ruin; “fall apart” implies separation into components B) “Come apart” suggests separation into pieces; “fall apart” suggests collapse into ruin C) They differ only in formality D) “Fall apart” is intransitive; “come apart” is transitive

5. “Apart from the noise, the hotel was perfect.” Why can this sentence be ambiguous? A) It cannot — “apart from” always means “except” B) “Apart from” can mean either except for (sole defect) or besides (one flaw among others) C) “Apart from” is informal D) It is ambiguous only in American English

6. Which sentence uses “a part / apart” correctly? A) She is apart of the team. B) Take a part the engine. C) She is a part of the team, though she stands apart from its culture. D) They are a part, worlds apart from each other.

Answer Key & Rationale

  1. B. Tell apart is idiomatic chiefly in the negative or with a modal (“can’t tell them apart”).
  2. B. The noun object normally sits between the verb and the particle; a pronoun must (“set them apart”).
  3. B. Drift apart is passive and gradual (loss of momentum); grow apart is developmental divergence.
  4. B. Come apart concerns pieces separating; fall apart concerns ruinous collapse.
  5. B. Apart from encodes both exclusion and addition, so context must disambiguate.
  6. C. A part of = a member; apart from = separate from. A and B misuse apart/a part; D is ungrammatical because worlds apart is predicative.

# Introduction: The Metaphor of "Apart"

The adverb and particle **apart** means *"into pieces"* or *"separated by distance or distinction."* Attached to verbs of placing, taking, coming, and tearing, it generates two broad conceptual families:

1. **Distinction and separation** — marking something as different, or keeping things at a distance (*set apart, tell apart, stand apart, keep apart, drift apart*).
2. **Disintegration and disassembly** — breaking something into its parts, literally or argumentatively (*come apart, fall apart, take apart, tear apart, pick apart*).

Like the *aside* family, these verbs are **near-synonyms with sharply different force**: *set apart* elevates, *take apart* dismantles, *tear apart* destroys. Choosing among them is a matter of **register, transitivity, and emotional temperature**.

```text
[ THE APART SPECTRUM ]

  DISTINCTION ──► set apart / tell apart ──► stand apart ──► a class apart
  (elevate, differentiate)                  (be conspicuous)   (idiomatic)

  SEPARATION ──► keep apart / hold apart ──► drift apart / grow apart
  (maintain distance)                       (lose closeness)

  DISINTEGRATION ──► come apart / fall apart ──► break apart / split apart
  (intransitive)                                (come to pieces)

  DISASSEMBLY ──► take apart / pull apart ──► pick apart / tear apart
  (transitive)                              (criticize, destroy)
```

## 1. Set Apart

The **flagship member of the family**, and the one most often required in formal prose. It has three distinct senses.

### A. To Distinguish or Elevate

To mark someone or something as **fundamentally different — usually superior — to others of the same class**:

* *"What **sets** elite translators **apart** is not vocabulary size but ear for register."*
* *"Her clarity of thought **set** her **apart** from her contemporaries."*
* *"A capacity for delayed gratification is what **sets** the species **apart**."*

* **Equivalents:** *distinguish, differentiate, elevate, single out, mark out.*
* **Polar opposites:** *assimilate, homogenize, blend in, merge with the crowd.*

> **Particle placement:** in this sense the object normally sits **between** *set* and *apart* — **set someone/something apart (from)**. *"Set apart the best students"* is possible but far less idiomatic than *"set the best students apart."*

### B. To Reserve or Consecrate

To **dedicate something to a special purpose**, often with a sacred or solemn colouring:

* *"The land was **set apart** for a nature reserve."*
* *"He was **set apart** for the priesthood."*
* *"These hours are **set apart** for deep work."*

* **Equivalents:** *consecrate, dedicate, earmark, reserve, hallow.*

> **Nuance:** this sense descends directly from the biblical and liturgical language of being *"set apart"* — chosen, sanctified, withdrawn from ordinary use. It explains why the phrase can sound either **ceremonial** or, in secular usage, merely **administrative** (*"funds set apart for repairs"*).

### C. Set Apart From — The Comparative Frame

The construction **X sets Y apart from Z** is the workhorse of evaluative writing:

* *"What **sets** this algorithm **apart from** its rivals is its worst-case guarantee."*

Note that the compared class is introduced by **from**, never *than*: *set apart ~~than~~* is a common error.

## 2. Tell Apart (and Know Apart)

To **distinguish two or more similar things by perceiving their differences**:

* *"I can't **tell** the twins **apart**."*
* *"Only an expert can **tell** the two vintages **apart**."*
* *"You can **tell** them **apart** by the stitching."*

### Usage Notes

* *Tell apart* is **overwhelmingly used in the negative or with a modal** (*can/can't, could/couldn't, hard to, difficult to*). Plain affirmative *"I tell them apart"* sounds unnatural outside a contrast (*"I can **tell** them **apart**, but my brother can't"*).
* The object must be **plural or a pair**: *tell the twins apart*, *tell them apart* — not *"tell the twin apart."*
* A rarer, more formal variant is **know apart**: *"The counterfeit is difficult to **know apart** from the original."*

> **Compare:** *tell apart* concerns **perception** (can I see the difference?); *set apart* concerns **status** (what makes it superior?). You *tell apart* similar things; you *set apart* exceptional ones.

## 3. Come Apart and Fall Apart

Both are **intransitive** — the subject disintegrates by itself — but their rhythm differs.

### Come Apart

To **separate into pieces**, often at a seam, joint, or weak point; also, of a plan or person, to **break down**:

* *"The old binding **came apart** in my hands."*
* *"The coalition **came apart** over the budget."*
* *"Under cross-examination, his story **came apart**."*

### Fall Apart

To **break into pieces and collapse**; figuratively, to **fail completely** or to suffer an emotional breakdown:

* *"The book **fell apart** after one reading."*
* *"Their marriage **fell apart**."*
* *"The whole plan **fell apart** at the last minute."*
* *"After the diagnosis, she **fell apart**."*

### Fixed Expressions

| Expression | Meaning |
| :--- | :--- |
| **Fall apart at the seams** | Fail at every weak point simultaneously |
| **Things fall apart** | An orderly system collapses (echoing Yeats and Achebe) |
| **Come apart at the seams** | Approach total collapse |

**Nuance:** *come apart* suggests **separation into components** (a mechanism, a case, an argument); *fall apart* suggests **collapse into ruin** (a structure, a relationship, a life). *Fall apart* is the stronger, more emotive choice.

---

## 4. Take Apart

To **disassemble something into its constituents**, either physically or — very commonly — **argumentatively**:

* *"He **took** the engine **apart** and rebuilt it."*
* *"The reviewer **took** the paper **apart**, paragraph by paragraph."*
* *"She **took apart** every claim in the manifesto."*

* **Equivalents:** *dismantle, disassemble, dissect, deconstruct, demolish (figurative).*
* **Polar opposites:** *assemble, put together, reassemble, construct.*

**Nuance:** *take apart* in the critical sense is **methodical**, not merely hostile — the dismantling proceeds piece by piece. It is the natural verb of rigorous critique and of *deconstruction*.

> **Related:** the noun **a takedown** (a devastating critical attack) and the adjective **take-apart** in technical writing (*"a take-apart teardown of the console"*).

## 5. Pull Apart, Tear Apart, Rip Apart

A trio of **forceful, transitive** verbs with overlapping but distinct imagery.

| Verb | Core image | Typical objects |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Pull apart** | Drawing two joined things away from each other | *combatants, factions, a couple, tissue* |
| **Tear apart** | Rending with violence; savaging | *a family, a reputation, an argument, a book* |
| **Rip apart** | Violent, abrupt rending | *clothing, a contract, an opponent's case* |

* *"Security **pulled** the two delegates **apart**."*
* *"The scandal **tore** the community **apart**."*
* *"Critics **tore apart** the film on release."*
* *"She **ripped apart** the proposal in open session."*

**Nuance:** *pull apart* is the **least violent** and most physical; it implies separating things that are gripped together, and it is the standard verb for **intervening in a fight**. *Tear apart* and *rip apart* belong to **emotional and rhetorical destruction** — a family *torn apart*, a thesis *ripped apart*. *Rip apart* is the most informal and the most aggressive.

## 6. Pick Apart

To **examine minutely in order to find faults** — the verb of hostile close reading:

* *"The auditors **picked apart** every line of the accounts."*
* *"He **picked apart** my reasoning until nothing was left."*
* *"Stop **picking apart** every word I say."*

* **Equivalents:** *nitpick, fault-find, scrutinize, cavil at, quibble over.*

> **Same idiom, different phrasing:** **pick holes in** and **pick to pieces** mean almost the same thing, as does the verb **fault** (*"I can't fault the logic"*). *Pick apart* implies **systematic, item-by-item** criticism rather than one sweeping verdict — compare *tear apart*, which delivers the verdict by force.

The **violent-separation** set.

| Verb | Sense | Example |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Break apart** | Come to pieces; force into pieces | *"The ice shelf **broke apart**."* |
| **Split apart** | Divide along a line, especially in groups | *"The party **split apart** over the vote."* |
| **Blow apart** | Destroy by explosion (often figurative) | *"The new evidence **blew apart** the prosecution's case."* |

## 8. Keeping Things Apart: Keep, Hold, Stand, Live, Drift, Grow

A subfamily about **maintaining or losing separation**.

### A. Keep Apart / Hold Apart / Keep (Them) Apart

* *"Keep the wet specimens **apart** from the dry ones."*
* *"The referee **held** the players **apart**."*
* *"It is wise to **keep** work and friendship **apart**."*

### B. Stand Apart — To Be Conspicuously Different

* *"One candidate **stands apart** from the rest in experience."*
* **Equivalents:** *stand out, be in a class of one, tower above.*

> **Nuance:** *stand apart* is **approving and evaluative** — it praises distinction. *Stand off* (withhold; keep at a distance) and *stand aloof* (be emotionally detached) are related but carry **coldness**, not admiration.

### C. Live Apart / Sleep Apart / Sit Apart — Physical Separation

* *"They have **lived apart** for a year."*
* *"The guests were **seated apart** from the family."*
* **Equivalents (relationship):** *separated, estranged* (*"a **legally separated** couple"*).

### D. Drift Apart / Grow Apart — The Slow Estrangement

These two are **near-synonyms** and are frequently used of friends, couples, and allies:

| Verb | Emphasis |
| :--- | :--- |
| **Drift apart** | Separation by **gradual, passive movement** — no one decides it |
| **Grow apart** | Separation through **divergent development** over time |

* *"We **drifted apart** after university."*
* *"The two industries have **grown apart** in culture and method."*

> **Nuance:** *drift* implies **loss of momentum** (like boats on a current); *grow* implies **divergence through development** (like branches). Neither implies hostility — that is what distinguishes them from *fall out* (a quarrel) and *break up* (an ending). To emphasise the intentional element there is the figurative **pull in different directions** and the verb **diverge**.

---

## 9. Apart From — Exception and Addition

The prepositional construction **apart from** has **two opposite readings**, and context alone disambiguates them.

### A. Except For (= with the exclusion of)

* *"**Apart from** the finale, the concert was flawless."*
* *"**Apart from** a minor injury, the squad is fit."*

### B. In Addition To (= as well as)

* *"**Apart from** teaching, she runs a small press."*
* *"**Apart from** the cost, there is the question of time."*

> **The classic ambiguity:** *"**Apart from** the noise, the hotel was perfect"* can mean **(a)** the noise was the sole defect, or **(b)** the noise was one flaw among others. When precision matters, use **except for** for (a) and **besides / as well as** for (b). *Apart from* is a British-favoured form; American English more often writes *aside from*.

### C. "Quite Apart From" — The Rhetorical Escalator

An emphatic frame that **adds a second, weightier consideration**: *"**Quite apart from** the expense, the plan is illegal."* Here *quite apart from* means roughly *"even leaving aside"* — compare the discourse frame *"setting aside X…"* in the *aside* family.

* **Equivalents:** *quite apart from, setting aside, leaving aside, aside from, independent of, not to mention.*

---

## 10. Adverbial "Apart": Worlds Apart, Poles Apart, A Class Apart

*Away from each other* — used in a set of **idiomatic complements** that denote extreme distance or distinction.

| Expression | Meaning |
| :--- | :--- |
| **Worlds apart** | Utterly different in outlook or quality |
| **Poles apart** | At opposite extremes (from the two poles) |
| **Miles apart** | Very far apart, literally or in agreement |
| **A class apart** | Superior to all others in its category |
| **A cut above** | Related idiom: distinctly better than |
| **Two sides of the same coin** | The reverse idiom: seemingly opposite, fundamentally linked |
| **Joking apart** | A discourse marker: "seriously now" |
| **Kidding aside / joking aside** | The *aside*-family equivalents |

* *"Their worldviews are **worlds apart**."*
* *"On this question the two economists are **poles apart**."*
* *"The finished instrument is **a class apart**."*
* *"**Joking apart**, we need a decision by Friday."*

> **Note:** *worlds apart* and *poles apart* are **predicative only** — they follow *be* or *stand*, they do not precede a noun. You cannot say *"a worlds-apart pair."*

## 12. Cases and Exceptions

* **Particle placement (separable vs. inseparable).** Most of these are **separable** when the object is a noun: *set the twins apart*, *take the engine apart*, *tear the family apart*. With a pronoun the particle **must** follow: *set **them** apart*, *take **it** apart* — never *"take apart it."*
* **The "a part" vs. "apart" trap.** *Apart* (one word) = separated; *a part* (two words) = a piece of a whole. So: *"She is **a part** of the team"* (a member) versus *"She stands **apart** from the team"* (separate). The errors *"apart of the team"* and *"take a part the engine"* both collapse this distinction. Compare the parallel trap in *alot / a lot*.
* **"Tell apart" needs a negative or modal.** *"I can tell them apart"* is acceptable as an assertion or contrast; *"I tell them apart"* is not idiomatic.
* **"Set apart" takes *from*, never *than*.** *"What sets it apart from the rest"* — not *"~~than~~ the rest."*
* **"Apart from" is ambiguous.** Use *except for* (exclusion) or *besides* (addition) when the reading matters.
* **"Apart" is predicative in idioms.** *Worlds apart*, *poles apart*, *a class apart* do not precede nouns.
* **"Fall apart" ≠ "fall out."** *Fall apart* is disintegration; *fall out* is a quarrel (*"they fell out over money"*) — entirely different, and a common learner confusion.
* **"Take apart" is not "take part."** *Take part in* means to participate (*"take part in the debate"*); *take apart* means to disassemble. The presence or absence of a space between *a* and *part* changes the meaning completely.
* **"Stand apart" and "set apart" are not interchangeable.** *Set apart* is usually **done by something** (*"her wit sets her apart"*); *stand apart* is a **state** (*"she stands apart from her peers"*).

## 13. Diagnostic Synthesis

> "What **set** the young engineer **apart** was her refusal to accept a result she could not explain. When a rival's dataset **didn't add up**, she **took** the pipeline **apart**, **picked apart** every transformation, and **tore apart** the claim in the review meeting — yet she never **told apart** a genuine discovery from a lucky accident without testing it herself. Meanwhile, the team she had left **drifted apart**; what had once been **worlds apart** in method were now **a class apart** in reputation."

## 14. Knowledge Retention Quiz

**1. Which verb is used almost exclusively in the negative or with a modal, and means "perceive the difference between"?**
A) set apart
B) tell apart
C) take apart
D) stand apart

**2. In "set apart," where does a noun object normally sit?**
A) After "apart" — *set apart the students*
B) Between *set* and *apart* — *set the students apart*
C) Before *set* — *the students set apart*
D) It cannot take a noun object

**3. Which pair correctly matches the nuance?**
A) *drift apart* = a deliberate decision; *grow apart* = a quarrel
B) *drift apart* = passive, gradual separation; *grow apart* = separation through divergent development
C) *drift apart* = a physical fight; *grow apart* = moving away
D) They are exact synonyms in every context

**4. What is the difference between "come apart" and "fall apart"?**
A) "Come apart" implies collapse into ruin; "fall apart" implies separation into components
B) "Come apart" suggests separation into pieces; "fall apart" suggests collapse into ruin
C) They differ only in formality
D) "Fall apart" is intransitive; "come apart" is transitive

**5. "Apart from the noise, the hotel was perfect." Why can this sentence be ambiguous?**
A) It cannot — "apart from" always means "except"
B) "Apart from" can mean either *except for* (sole defect) or *besides* (one flaw among others)
C) "Apart from" is informal
D) It is ambiguous only in American English

**6. Which sentence uses "a part / apart" correctly?**
A) She is apart of the team.
B) Take a part the engine.
C) She is a part of the team, though she stands apart from its culture.
D) They are a part, worlds apart from each other.

### Answer Key & Rationale
1. **B.** *Tell apart* is idiomatic chiefly in the negative or with a modal (*"can't tell them apart"*).
2. **B.** The noun object normally sits between the verb and the particle; a pronoun must (*"set them apart"*).
3. **B.** *Drift apart* is passive and gradual (loss of momentum); *grow apart* is developmental divergence.
4. **B.** *Come apart* concerns pieces separating; *fall apart* concerns ruinous collapse.
5. **B.** *Apart from* encodes both exclusion and addition, so context must disambiguate.
6. **C.** *A part of* = a member; *apart from* = separate from. A and B misuse *apart/a part*; D is ungrammatical because *worlds apart* is predicative.
