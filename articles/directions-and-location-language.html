Introduction: Locating Ourselves

Every language must answer two spatial questions: Where is it? and Which way? English answers with an intricate system of compass forms, directional adverbs, prepositions, and idioms of place. This guide maps that system, from the grammar of north to the idiom of around the corner.

1. The Compass and Its Four Forms

For each cardinal direction, English has a family of related forms. Mastering the differences is the key to sounding natural.

Base Adjective (relational) Adjective (directional) Adverb (direction) Noun phrase
North northern northerly northward(s) the north
South southern southerly southward(s) the south
East eastern easterly eastward(s) the east
West western westerly westward(s) the west

Using Them Correctly

  • northern / southern / eastern / western describe a location or region: “northern Italy,” “the western coast.”
  • northerly / southerly / easterly / westerly describe a direction (often of wind): “a northerly wind,” “a southerly course.”
  • northward(s) / southward(s) are adverbs of motion: “the birds flew southward(s).”
  • the north / the south are noun phrases: “the north of England.”

The Critical Error: “to north”

❌ “The town lies to north of the city.” ✅ “The town lies to the north of the city.” ✅ “The town lies north of the city.” ✅ “The town lies to the north.”

Rule: You may say north of (no article) or to the north of (with the article) — but never to north of.

Intermediate directions: northeast (noun), northeastern (adjective), to the northeast of. Note the hyphen-free spellings are now standard: northeast, southwest.

2. Regional Idioms of the Compass

In both British and American English, compass words carry cultural associations with regions:

Expression Meaning
up north Toward/to the north (often connoting a colder, industrial, or rural region)
down south Toward/to the south (often connoting warmth, cities, or a capital)
out west Toward the western frontier (AmE)
back east Toward the eastern establishment (AmE)
the frozen north Humorous: a very cold northern place

Note: up and down here are idiomatic, not literal — down south does not mean descending. On maps, though, north is drawn up, which reinforces up north.

3. Directional Adverbs

Pair Meaning
forwards / backwards Toward the front / the back
sideways To one side
upwards / downwards Toward a higher / lower position
inwards / outwards Toward the inside / outside
onwards Forward in time or space
homewards Toward home
clockwise / anticlockwise (BrE) In the direction of a clock’s hands / opposite
lengthwise / crosswise Along the length / across
left / right Lateral direction
straight ahead Directly forward

Spelling: British English prefers -wards (backwards, forwards); American English often drops the -s (backward, forward). Both are correct.

4. “Around the Corner”

This idiom has two distinct meanings — one spatial, one temporal:

A. Nearby (Spatial)

  • “The bakery is just around the corner.”
  • “He lives around the corner from us.”

B. Imminent (Temporal)

  • “Spring is around the corner.”
  • “A breakthrough may be around the corner.”

Variants

  • round the corner (British, informal): the same as around the corner.
  • just around the bend (AmE): nearby; also used for imminent change.
  • on the horizon: imminent (often of change or trouble).

Nuance: The temporal use of around the corner implies something is close at hand but not yet arrived — close and expected.

5. Prepositions of Place and Direction (Quick Reference)

Preposition Use Example
in Enclosed space / region “in the room,” “in the north”
on Surface / line / transport “on the coast,” “on the table”
at Point / specific location “at the corner,” “at the bridge”
near / close to / by / beside Proximity “near the station”
to / toward(s) Direction of motion “to the north,” “toward the door”
into / onto Motion to inside / onto a surface “into the house”
through / across / along Path of motion “through the forest”
beyond / past / over / under Relative position “beyond the hills”
around / about Encirclement / approximation “around the city”

Corner distinctions: at the corner (a point), on the corner (of streets; AmE), in the corner (enclosed inner corner of a room).

6. Positional and Locational Idioms

Idiom Meaning
in the vicinity Nearby, in the area
in the neighbourhood (of) Near, approximately
on the outskirts At the edge of a town
at the heart of In the centre of
at a crossroads At a decisive point (also literal)
in the middle of nowhere Far from anywhere
off the beaten track / path Remote, seldom visited
the back of beyond (BrE) A very remote place
the sticks (informal) Rural, remote area
the four corners of the earth Everywhere
from all corners of the world From everywhere
to the four winds Scattered in all directions
every which way In all directions, chaotically
hither and thither Back and forth, in various directions
on the horizon Imminent; in view

7. Nautical and Aviation Directions (For Completeness)

Term Meaning
port / starboard Left / right side of a ship (facing forward)
fore / aft Toward the front / back of a ship
bow / stern The front / back of a ship
windward / leeward Toward / away from the wind
abeam At right angles to the ship’s length
astern Behind the vessel

8. Cases and Exceptions

  • “to north” is never correct — always to the north or north of.
  • “up north” / “down south” are cultural, not literal — they do not describe elevation.
  • “around the corner” shifts between place and time — context decides.
  • “in the corner” vs. “on/at the corner”: in an enclosed corner (of a room); at/on a street corner.
  • “northerly” describes direction (wind), “northern” describes region — not interchangeable.
  • -wards vs. -ward: both correct; British favours -wards.

9. Model Sentences

“The old mill lies to the north of the village, just around the corner from the crossroads. After the harvest, the workers scattered to the four winds — some up north, some down south — until only the miller remained, off the beaten track, waiting for the spring that was, at last, around the corner.”

10. Knowledge Retention Quiz

1. Which is grammatically correct? A) The town lies to north of the city. B) The town lies to the north of the city.

2. Which word describes a direction (as of wind), rather than a region? A) northern B) northerly C) the north D) northbound

3. “Spring is around the corner” uses the idiom in which sense? A) Spatial (nearby) B) Temporal (imminent) C) Nautical D) Literal corner

4. “Down south” idiomatically means: A) literally descending B) toward the south (a cultural idiom, not literal elevation) C) toward the north D) underground

5. Which is correct for the inner angle where two walls meet? A) on the corner B) in the corner C) at the corner D) by the corner

Answer Key & Rationale

  1. B. Use to the north of or north of — never to north of.
  2. B. Northerly describes direction; northern describes a region.
  3. B. Here around the corner means imminent.
  4. B. Down south is a cultural idiom, not a description of elevation.
  5. B. In the corner describes an enclosed inner corner.

# Introduction: Locating Ourselves

Every language must answer two spatial questions: *Where is it?* and *Which way?* English answers with an intricate system of **compass forms, directional adverbs, prepositions, and idioms of place**. This guide maps that system, from the grammar of *north* to the idiom of *around the corner*.

---

## 1. The Compass and Its Four Forms

For each cardinal direction, English has a family of related forms. Mastering the differences is the key to sounding natural.

| Base | Adjective (relational) | Adjective (directional) | Adverb (direction) | Noun phrase |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **North** | northern | northerly | northward(s) | the north |
| **South** | southern | southerly | southward(s) | the south |
| **East** | eastern | easterly | eastward(s) | the east |
| **West** | western | westerly | westward(s) | the west |

### Using Them Correctly
* **northern / southern / eastern / western** describe a *location or region*: *"northern Italy," "the western coast."*
* **northerly / southerly / easterly / westerly** describe a *direction* (often of wind): *"a northerly wind," "a southerly course."*
* **northward(s) / southward(s)** are adverbs of motion: *"the birds flew southward(s)."*
* **the north / the south** are noun phrases: *"the north of England."*

### The Critical Error: "to north"
> ❌ *"The town lies to north of the city."*
> ✅ *"The town lies **to the north of** the city."*
> ✅ *"The town lies **north of** the city."*
> ✅ *"The town lies **to the north**."*

**Rule:** You may say *north of* (no article) or *to the north of* (with the article) — but **never** *to north of*.

Intermediate directions: *northeast* (noun), *northeastern* (adjective), *to the northeast of*. Note the hyphen-free spellings are now standard: *northeast*, *southwest*.
