Introduction: The Shape of Time

If the first article in this pair mapped the axis of time (before and after), this one maps its shape: how long things last (duration), the gaps between them (intervals), and the picturesque idioms English uses to talk about timing. Together they form the practical vocabulary of time in English.

1. Words for Duration

Word Nuance Example
Duration The length of time something lasts (neutral/technical) “the duration of the flight”
Span The full extent from beginning to end; also lifespan/attention span “a career spanning four decades”
Stretch A continuous, often notable period “a long stretch of bad weather”
Spell A short or indefinite period, often of weather or rest “a spell of sunshine,” “a dizzy spell”
Period Any length of time; also a school lesson; menstruation “a period of recovery”
Term A fixed length of time; a school semester; a prison sentence “a two-year term”
Tenure The period of holding an office or position “during her tenure as mayor”
Stint A fixed period of work or duty “a stint in the army”
Epoch / Era / Age A distinct historical period “the digital era,” “the Ice Age”
Eon (aeon) An immeasurably long period “it felt like an eon”
Lifetime The duration of a life “once in a lifetime”

Nuance ladder of length:

moment ─► spell ─► stretch / stint ─► period / term ─► span ─► era ─► epoch ─► eon
(seconds) (short)   (notable)          (formal/fixed)   (full)  (historic)(geologic)(vast)

2. Words for Intervals and Pauses

Word Nuance Example
Interval The time between two events (neutral) “a 20-minute interval”
Gap A break or unfilled space in time “a gap between jobs”
Interlude A pleasant or calming pause between two periods “a brief interlude of peace”
Hiatus A break in continuity, often an unplanned stop “the band went on hiatus”
Pause A short stop “a pause for breath”
Recess A temporary suspension (school, parliament, court) “the summer recess”
Respite A short rest from something difficult “a respite from the noise”
Reprieve A temporary relief, especially from punishment “a last-minute reprieve”
Lull A temporary calm between periods of activity “a lull in the storm”
Breather An informal short rest “let’s take a breather”
Armistice / truce A formal suspension of fighting “the armistice of 1918”

Nuance notes: * Hiatus implies an interruption in something that was ongoing — often longer than expected. * Interlude is a pleasant or ornamenting pause; hiatus is more neutral or disruptive. * Respite and reprieve both carry a sense of relief from difficulty; reprieve often implies narrowly avoiding something bad. * Lull implies deceptively temporary calm — often before renewed activity.

3. Interim, Meantime, Meanwhile

  • Interim (noun/adjective): the time before something is settled — “an interim government,” “in the interim.”
  • Meantime: the intervening time — “in the meantime” (very common).
  • Meanwhile: at the same time, elsewhere — “Meanwhile, in Paris…” (a narrative bridge).

4. Verbs of Passing Time

Verb Meaning Example
Elapse (of time) to pass by (formal, time-only) “three years elapsed”
Pass (of time) to go by (neutral) “the hours passed slowly”
Lapse (of time) to slip by; also to expire “several months lapsed”
Wear on (of time) to pass slowly and tediously “as the night wore on”
Drag (on) (of time) to pass slowly “the lecture dragged”
Fly (by) (of time) to pass quickly “the years flew by”
Span (of events) to extend across “a life spanning a century”
Eke out to make a limited amount last “eke out the supplies”

Note: Elapse is used only of time; lapse also means to expire or slip into a state. Cross-reference the article on the lapse family.

5. The Great Time Idioms

Idiom Meaning
In the nick of time Just in time; at the last moment
At the eleventh hour At the last possible moment
Around the clock Twenty-four hours a day, continuously
Once in a blue moon Very rarely
In the long run Over a long period; ultimately
In the short term / long term Over a brief / extended period
A race against time An urgent struggle to finish before a deadline
The test of time The proof of lasting value
A matter of time An inevitable future occurrence
Time and (time) again Repeatedly
At the drop of a hat Immediately, without hesitation
Behind the times Old-fashioned, outdated
Ahead of its time Advanced beyond the current era
The sands of time The passing of time (literary)
Time flies Time passes quickly
Kill time To occupy oneself while waiting
Better late than never Doing something late is better than not at all
In the meantime During the intervening period
At a moment’s notice With very little warning

6. Precision Markers: “Time” Prepositions

Phrase Meaning
in time Before it is too late
on time Punctually, at the scheduled time
at the same time Simultaneously
by the time Before or when (a deadline/event)
from time to time Occasionally
for the time being Temporarily, for now
in no time Very quickly
at all times Continuously, always
ahead of time Earlier than required
behind schedule Later than planned

Classic distinction: “in time” = before a deadline (“we arrived in time for the start”); “on time” = punctually (“the train was on time”).

7. Cases and Exceptions

  • Elapse vs. lapse: elapse is strictly time passing; lapse also means to expire or to slip into a state.
  • Hiatus vs. interlude: a hiatus interrupts; an interlude refreshes.
  • Reprieve vs. respite: a reprieve postpones a punishment; a respite gives relief from difficulty.
  • “In the long run” vs. “in the long term”: near-synonyms, but in the long run is idiomatically fixed and slightly more casual.
  • “Once in a blue moon” is genuinely rare — do not use it for merely uncommon events.
  • “Around the clock” means continuously through the day and night, not merely “all day.”

8. Model Sentences

“After a hiatus of nearly a decade, the orchestra returned for a brief interlude of touring. The rehearsals dragged at first, but as the deadline closed in, the musicians worked around the clock; and in the nick of time, on the eve of the opening night, the score was ready.”

9. Knowledge Retention Quiz

1. Which word is used exclusively of time passing? A) lapse B) elapse C) pass out D) wear

2. Which word means a temporary, pleasant pause between two periods? A) hiatus B) interlude C) gap D) recess

3. What is the difference between “in time” and “on time”? A) They are identical. B) “In time” = before it is too late; “on time” = punctually. C) “On time” = before it is too late; “in time” = punctually. D) Neither is idiomatic.

4. Which idiom means “very rarely”? A) around the clock B) once in a blue moon C) in the nick of time D) at the drop of a hat

5. “Respite” most closely means: A) a permanent end B) a short rest or relief from something difficult C) a formal treaty D) a sudden beginning

Answer Key & Rationale

  1. B. Elapse is used only of time.
  2. B. Interlude suggests a pleasant pause; hiatus suggests interruption.
  3. B. In time = ahead of a deadline; on time = punctual.
  4. B. Once in a blue moon = very rarely.
  5. B. Respite is a temporary relief from difficulty.
