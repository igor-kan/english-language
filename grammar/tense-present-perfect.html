Introduction

The present perfect is the tense that gives English learners the most trouble — and for good reason. It does not simply describe the past; it links it to the present. An event is finished, but its relevance, result, or continuation is felt now. The present perfect answers the question “How does the past bear on the present?”

Formation

Subject + have/has + past participle (V3)

Affirmative Negative Interrogative
I / you / we / they I have finished I have not (haven’t) finished Have I finished?
he / she / it She has finished She has not (hasn’t) finished Has she finished?

Core Uses

1. Past Action with Present Result

  • “I have lost my keys.” (So I can’t get in now.)
  • “She has broken her arm.” (It is broken now.)

2. Life Experience (unspecified time)

  • “He has visited Japan three times.”
  • “I have never eaten oysters.”

3. Unfinished States or Durations Continuing to Now

  • “We have lived here for ten years.” (We still do.)
  • “I have known her since 2018.”

4. Recent Past with just, already, yet

  • “They have just arrived.”
  • “I haven’t finished yet.”
  • “She has already left.”

5. Repeated Actions Up to Now

  • “I have called him five times this morning.”

The Time-Marker System

The present perfect rejects finished-time expressions (yesterday, last year, in 1999, ago) and embraces open-time markers:

Accepts (present-perfect markers) Rejects (past-simple markers)
ever, never, already, yet yesterday, last night
just, recently, lately last week, in 2010
for, since, so far two days ago
this week/month/year then, when?

The Critical Contrast: Present Perfect vs. Past Simple

This is the fault line where most errors occur. The choice depends on whether the time period is finished or open, and whether the focus is on present relevance or a specific past moment.

Present Perfect Past Simple
Time is unspecified or unfinished Specific, finished time
“I have seen that film.” “I saw that film last night.”
Present relevance Completed past event
“He has gone to Rome.” (still there / relevant) “He went to Rome in May.” (just a fact)
“I have lived here for years.” (still) “I lived there for years.” (no longer)

❌ “I have seen him yesterday.” → ✅ “I saw him yesterday.” ❌ “Did you ever eat sushi?” (AmE) / ✅ “Have you ever eaten sushi?” (standard BrE)

British vs. American Usage

American English frequently uses the past simple where British English prefers the present perfect: * BrE: “I’ve just eaten.” / AmE: “I just ate.” * BrE: “Have you seen it yet?” / AmE: “Did you see it yet?”

Present Perfect vs. Present Perfect Continuous

Present Perfect Present Perfect Continuous
Focus on result / completion Focus on duration / process
“I have painted the wall.” (done) “I have been painting the wall.” (ongoing / recently)
“She has read the book.” (finished) “She has been reading the book.” (not finished)

Nuances

  • “Have been to” vs. “have gone to”: “She has been to Paris” = she visited and returned; “She has gone to Paris” = she is there now.
  • With this: “This is the best meal I have ever had.”
  • Intensifiers: “It’s the first time I have flown.”

Common Errors

❌ “I have finished it two hours ago.” → ✅ “I finished it two hours ago.” ❌ “He has been to Rome last year.” → ✅ “He went to Rome last year.” ❌ “I am living here since 2020.” → ✅ “I have lived here since 2020.”

Model Sentences

“Over the past decade she has published four books and has never once missed a deadline — a record that has earned her the trust of the entire board.”

Knowledge Check

1. Which sentence is correct? A) I have seen him yesterday. B) I saw him yesterday. C) I have saw him yesterday.

2. Choose the correct sentence: A) I am knowing her for ten years. B) I know her since ten years. C) I have known her for ten years.

3. When is the present perfect preferred over the past simple? A) When a specific finished time is mentioned. B) When the time is unspecified or the past event has present relevance. C) Never; they are interchangeable.

Answer Key

  1. B. Yesterday is a finished-time marker, requiring the past simple.
  2. C. Duration continuing to the present requires the present perfect.
  3. B. The present perfect links an unspecified past to a present result or continuation.

## Introduction

The present perfect is the tense that gives English learners the most trouble — and for good reason. It does not simply describe the past; it links it to the present. An event is *finished*, but its relevance, result, or continuation is felt **now**. The present perfect answers the question *"How does the past bear on the present?"*

## Formation

**Subject + have/has + past participle (V3)**

| | Affirmative | Negative | Interrogative |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **I / you / we / they** | I **have finished** | I **have not (haven't) finished** | **Have** I **finished**? |
| **he / she / it** | She **has finished** | She **has not (hasn't) finished** | **Has** she **finished**? |

## Core Uses

### 1. Past Action with Present Result
* *"I **have lost** my keys."* (So I can't get in now.)

### 2. Life Experience (unspecified time)

* *"They **have just arrived**."*
* *"I **haven't finished** yet."*
* *"She **has already left**."*

### 5. Repeated Actions Up to Now
* *"I **have called** him five times this morning."*

| Accepts (present-perfect markers) | Rejects (past-simple markers) |
| :--- | :--- |

This is the fault line where most errors occur. The choice depends on whether the time period is **finished** or **open**, and whether the focus is on **present relevance** or a **specific past moment**.

| Present Perfect | Past Simple |
| :--- | :--- |
| Time is unspecified or unfinished | Specific, finished time |
| *"I **have seen** that film."* | *"I **saw** that film last night."* |
| Present relevance | Completed past event |
| *"He **has gone** to Rome."* (still there / relevant) | *"He **went** to Rome in May."* (just a fact) |

* BrE: *"I've just eaten."* / AmE: *"I just ate."*
* BrE: *"Have you seen it yet?"* / AmE: *"Did you see it yet?"*

| Present Perfect | Present Perfect Continuous |
| :--- | :--- |
| Focus on **result / completion** | Focus on **duration / process** |
| *"I **have painted** the wall."* (done) | *"I **have been painting** the wall."* (ongoing / recently) |

## Knowledge Check
