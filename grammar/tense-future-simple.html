Will vs. going to, and the full inventory of English future forms.

Introduction

English has no true future tense inflection — unlike Spanish (hablaré) or French (parlerai). Instead, the future is expressed through modal and semi-modal constructions: will, be going to, the present simple, and the present continuous. The future simple (will + base verb) is the most common, but choosing correctly among these forms conveys crucial nuance about intention, planning, and evidence.

Formation

Subject + will + base verb (invariable for all subjects)

Affirmative Negative Interrogative
All subjects They will leave They will not (won’t) leave Will they leave?

In formal British English, shall may replace will with I/we: “I shall return.”

Core Uses of “Will”

1. Predictions and Forecasts

  • “The economy will grow by 2% next year.”
  • “You will love this film.”

2. Spontaneous Decisions (made at the moment of speaking)

  • “I’m tired — I will just skip dessert.”
  • “The phone’s ringing. I will get it.”

3. Promises, Offers, Threats, and Refusals

  • “I will help you move on Saturday.” (offer)
  • “I will never speak to him again.” (promise/threat)
  • “The engine won’t start.” (refusal of a thing)

4. Requests

  • “Will you close the window, please?”

5. Future Facts and Conditional Results

  • “The concert will begin at 8 p.m.”
  • “If you heat ice, it will melt.” (first conditional)

Core Uses of “Be Going To”

1. Prior Plans and Intentions

The decision existed before the moment of speaking: * “We are going to visit my parents this weekend.” (already planned) * “She is going to start a new job in May.”

2. Predictions Based on Present Evidence

  • “Look at those clouds — it is going to rain.”
  • “He’s losing his balance! He is going to fall!”

Will vs. Be Going To

Will Be Going To
Spontaneous decision Premeditated plan
“I’ll have the soup.” (deciding now) “I’m going to have the soup.” (decided earlier)
Prediction (opinion/belief) Prediction (visible evidence)
“I think it will rain tomorrow.” “It’s going to rain — look at the sky.”

The Complete Future Inventory

Form Use Example
Will + base Predictions, spontaneous decisions “It will be cold tomorrow.”
Be going to Plans, evidence-based predictions “We’re going to move.”
Present continuous Definite arrangements “I’m meeting Sam at six.”
Present simple Timetables, schedules “The train leaves at nine.”
Be about to Immediate future “The film is about to start.”
Be to Official plans, instructions “The Queen is to visit Canada.”
Shall Offers/suggestions (I/we) “Shall I open the window?”

Nuances

  • “Shall I / Shall we” is the standard way to make offers and suggestions in British English: “Shall we go?”
  • Deduction (not future): Will can express present assumptions: “That will be the postman” (= I assume that is the postman).
  • Future in the past: For past viewpoints, use would or was going to: “He said he would call,” “She was going to resign.”

Common Errors

❌ “I will to call you.” → ✅ “I will call you.” (no to after will) ❌ “The phone is ringing — I’m going to get it.” (suggests prior plan) → ✅ “I’ll get it.” (spontaneous) ❌ “When I will see him, I’ll tell him.” → ✅ “When I see him, I’ll tell him.”

Model Sentences

“The board is going to announce the merger on Monday; the markets will likely react nervously, and analysts will be watching every word.”

Knowledge Check

1. Which expresses a spontaneous decision? A) I’m going to buy a car next month. B) I’ll have the chicken.

2. Which predicts based on visible evidence? A) I think it will rain. B) Look at the clouds — it’s going to rain.

3. What is the correct form: “When she ___ (arrive), we’ll eat.” A) will arrive B) arrives

Answer Key

  1. B. Will marks a decision made at the moment of speaking.
  2. B. Going to is used for predictions grounded in present evidence.
  3. B. Time clauses take the present simple, not will.

## Introduction

English has **no true future tense inflection** — unlike Spanish (*hablaré*) or French (*parlerai*). Instead, the future is expressed through **modal and semi-modal constructions**: *will*, *be going to*, the present simple, and the present continuous. The future simple (*will* + base verb) is the most common, but choosing correctly among these forms conveys crucial nuance about intention, planning, and evidence.

## Formation

**Subject + will + base verb** (invariable for all subjects)

| | Affirmative | Negative | Interrogative |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **All subjects** | They **will leave** | They **will not (won't) leave** | **Will** they **leave**? |

In formal British English, *shall* may replace *will* with *I/we*: *"I **shall** return."*

## Core Uses of "Will"

### 1. Predictions and Forecasts
* *"The economy **will grow** by 2% next year."*
* *"You **will love** this film."*

### 2. Spontaneous Decisions (made at the moment of speaking)
* *"I'm tired — I **will** just **skip** dessert."*
* *"The phone's ringing. I **will get** it."*

### 3. Promises, Offers, Threats, and Refusals
* *"I **will help** you move on Saturday."* (offer)
* *"I **will never** speak to him again."* (promise/threat)
* *"The engine **won't start**."* (refusal of a thing)

### 4. Requests
* *"**Will** you close the window, please?"*

### 5. Future Facts and Conditional Results
* *"The concert **will begin** at 8 p.m."*
* *"If you heat ice, it **will melt**."* (first conditional)

## Core Uses of "Be Going To"

### 1. Prior Plans and Intentions
The decision existed before the moment of speaking:
* *"We **are going to visit** my parents this weekend."* (already planned)
* *"She **is going to start** a new job in May."*

### 2. Predictions Based on Present Evidence
* *"Look at those clouds — it **is going to rain**."*
* *"He's losing his balance! He **is going to fall**!"*

## Will vs. Be Going To

| Will | Be Going To |
| :--- | :--- |
| Spontaneous decision | Premeditated plan |
| *"I'll have the soup."* (deciding now) | *"I'm going to have the soup."* (decided earlier) |
| Prediction (opinion/belief) | Prediction (visible evidence) |
| *"I think it will rain tomorrow."* | *"It's going to rain — look at the sky."* |

## The Complete Future Inventory

| Form | Use | Example |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Will + base** | Predictions, spontaneous decisions | *"It will be cold tomorrow."* |
| **Be going to** | Plans, evidence-based predictions | *"We're going to move."* |
| **Present continuous** | Definite arrangements | *"I'm meeting Sam at six."* |
| **Present simple** | Timetables, schedules | *"The train leaves at nine."* |
| **Be about to** | Immediate future | *"The film is about to start."* |
| **Be to** | Official plans, instructions | *"The Queen is to visit Canada."* |
| **Shall** | Offers/suggestions (I/we) | *"Shall I open the window?"* |

## Nuances

* **"Shall I / Shall we"** is the standard way to make offers and suggestions in British English: *"**Shall we** go?"*
* **Deduction (not future):** *Will* can express present assumptions: *"That **will be** the postman"* (= I assume that is the postman).
* **Future in the past:** For past viewpoints, use *would* or *was going to*: *"He said he **would call**,"* *"She **was going to** resign."*

## Common Errors

> ❌ *"I will to call you."* → ✅ *"I **will call** you."* (no *to* after *will*)
> ❌ *"The phone is ringing — I'm going to get it."* (suggests prior plan) → ✅ *"I'**ll** get it."* (spontaneous)
> ❌ *"When I will see him, I'll tell him."* → ✅ *"When I **see** him, I'll tell him."*

## Model Sentences

> *"The board **is going to announce** the merger on Monday; the markets **will likely react** nervously, and analysts **will be watching** every word."*

## Knowledge Check

**1. Which expresses a spontaneous decision?**
A) I'm going to buy a car next month.
B) I'll have the chicken.

**2. Which predicts based on visible evidence?**
A) I think it will rain.
B) Look at the clouds — it's going to rain.

**3. What is the correct form: "When she ___ (arrive), we'll eat."**
A) will arrive
B) arrives

### Answer Key
1. **B.** *Will* marks a decision made at the moment of speaking.
2. **B.** *Going to* is used for predictions grounded in present evidence.
3. **B.** Time clauses take the present simple, not *will*.
