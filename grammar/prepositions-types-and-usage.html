Introduction: The Smallest Giants of English

Prepositions are short, unassuming words — at, in, on, of, to, by, for, with — yet they carry an enormous share of a sentence’s meaning. There are only about 150 of them, making prepositions a closed class: new ones are rarely coined, and the same handful recur with bewildering frequency. They are also the most idiomatic words in English. Logic alone will not tell you why we say good at but interested in, depend on but consist of. Prepositions must be learned as collocations as much as rules.

A preposition links a noun, pronoun, or gerund (its object) to another part of the sentence, expressing a relationship of place, time, direction, cause, manner, agency, or possession.

1. The Four Formal Categories

A. Simple (one-word) Prepositions

at  by  for  from  in  of  on  to  with  without  over  under
up  down  off  near  past  since  until  through  between  among

B. Compound (two-word) Prepositions

Formed by adding a prefix to a simple preposition:

into   onto   upon   within   without   throughout   alongside
amidst  amongst  toward  towards  outside  inside  notwithstanding

C. Complex (multi-word / phrasal) Prepositions

Fixed phrases functioning as a single preposition:

in front of     on behalf of     in spite of      because of
due to          owing to         apart from       according to
by means of     with regard to   in terms of      next to
out of          as for           except for       instead of
prior to        subsequent to    in addition to   with respect to

D. Double Prepositions

Two prepositions working together:

from behind   from under   from beneath   from among   until after   out of

“He emerged from behind the curtain.” / “She waited until after the ceremony.”

2. Prepositions of Place and Location

The at / in / on System

The three most important place prepositions form a system based on how we conceive of the location:

Preposition Concept Examples
at A point or location seen abstractly at the door, at the station, at the party, at 10 Downing Street
in An enclosed space, area, or volume in the box, in London, in the car, in the water
on A surface; a line; public transport on the table, on the wall, on the bus, on the third floor

Subtle difference: “I’ll meet you at the station” (a meeting point) vs. “I’ll wait in the station” (inside the building) vs. “I’m on the train” (aboard).

Other Spatial Prepositions

Preposition Relationship
above / over Higher than (over often implies covering or movement across)
below / under / underneath / beneath Lower than (underneath emphasizes being directly beneath; beneath is literary)
between In the space separating two things
among Surrounded by three or more
beside / next to / by / near At the side of; close to
behind / in front of To the rear / to the front of
opposite / across from Facing
along / around / past / beyond / through Following a length, encircling, passing, further than, penetrating

3. Prepositions of Time

The at / on / in System

Preposition Use Examples
at Clock times, points in the day, festivals at 6 o’clock, at noon, at night, at Christmas
on Days and dates on Monday, on 4 July, on my birthday, on the weekend (AmE)
in Months, years, seasons, longer periods, parts of the day in July, in 2026, in winter, in the morning

Other Temporal Prepositions

Preposition Meaning Example
for Duration for three hours
since From a past point until now since 2020
during Within a period (not its length) during the film
until / till Up to a point (continuation) until midnight
by No later than a deadline by Friday
before / after Earlier / later than before dawn, after lunch
within Inside a limit of time within an hour
throughout For the whole of a period throughout the year
between … and A span with two limits between 9 and 5

Critical contrast — “by” vs. “until”: “Finish it by Friday” = deadline (finish at or before Friday). “Wait until Friday” = continue waiting up to Friday.

4. Prepositions of Movement and Direction

to      into     onto     toward(s)   through   across   along
around  past     over     under       up        down      out of
off     from     back to
  • “She walked to the door, through the hall, and out of the building.”
  • “The cat jumped onto the table and off again.”
  • He ran toward the exit, past the guards, and down the stairs.”*

Note the distinction between in/into (location vs. movement): “She is in the room” (location) vs. “She walked into the room” (movement).

5. Prepositions of Other Semantic Roles

Role Prepositions Example
Agent by “written by Orwell”
Instrument with “cut with a knife”
Cause / reason because of, due to, owing to, on account of, thanks to, for “cancelled due to rain”
Purpose for, for the sake of “a tool for cutting”
Manner / means by, with, in, without “travelled by train”
Possession / measure of “a cup of tea”
Topic / regard about, on, of, regarding, concerning, over “a book about Rome”
Concession despite, in spite of, notwithstanding “despite the storm”

6. Prepositional Phrases and Their Functions

A preposition plus its object forms a prepositional phrase, which can function as:

  • Adjectival (modifying a noun): “the man in the hat,” “a matter of principle.”
  • Adverbial (modifying a verb, adjective, or the whole clause): “She left in a hurry,” “famous for her wit.”

7. Prepositions vs. Particles vs. Adverbs

The same word can play different roles. Compare up:

Sentence Function of up
“She climbed up the hill.” Preposition (has object the hill)
“She looked up the word.” Particle (part of phrasal verb; object the word)
“She looked up.” Adverb (no object)

Test: A preposition is always followed by its object; a particle is bound to the verb and changes its meaning; an adverb is independent.

8. Grammatical Rules and Persistent Myths

  • Ending a sentence with a preposition is perfectly grammatical. English “strands” prepositions: “That’s the book I told you about.” The formal alternative (pied-piping) is “the book about which I told you,” which is appropriate in very formal prose but not required.
  • Prepositions govern gerunds, not infinitives: ✅ “interested in learning,” ❌ “interested in to learn.”
  • Use the object case after a preposition: ✅ “between you and me,” ❌ “between you and I.”
  • A preposition never takes a verb as its object (except in the gerund form).
  • Avoid unnecessary prepositions: ❌ “discuss about the plan” → ✅ “discuss the plan.”

9. Common Errors

❌ “I’ll meet you in the corner of the street.” → ✅ “on the corner” / “at the corner.” ❌ “I have been here since three hours.” → ✅ “for three hours.” ❌ “Finish it until Friday.” → ✅ “Finish it by Friday.” ❌ “Married with him.” → ✅ “Married to him.” ❌ “Different than” (AmE acceptable) → ✅ “Different from” (standard BrE) / “different to” (BrE informal).

10. Knowledge Check

1. Which sentence uses the correct preposition? A) I’ll meet you in the corner. B) I’ll meet you at the corner.

2. Choose the correct form: “She has lived here ___ 2015.” A) for B) since

3. When do we use “by” rather than “until”? A) To express a deadline (“finish by 5”). B) To express continuous waiting.

4. Which is the object case? A) between you and I B) between you and me

5. In “she looked up the word,” what is “up”? A) A preposition B) A particle C) A noun

Answer Key

  1. B. At the corner treats the location as a point; in the corner suggests an enclosed inner corner.
  2. B. Since marks a starting point continuing to now; for would require a duration (for ten years).
  3. A. By marks a deadline; until marks continuation up to a point.
  4. B. Prepositions take the object case (me).
  5. B. Up is a particle forming the phrasal verb look up.

| Preposition | Concept | Examples |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |

### Other Temporal Prepositions

| Preposition | Meaning | Example |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **for** | Duration | *for three hours* |

A preposition plus its object forms a **prepositional phrase**, which can function as:

## 8. Grammatical Rules and Persistent Myths

* **Ending a sentence with a preposition is perfectly grammatical.** English "strands" prepositions: *"That's the book I told you **about**."* The formal alternative (pied-piping) is *"the book **about which** I told you,"* which is appropriate in very formal prose but not required.
* **Prepositions govern gerunds, not infinitives:** ✅ *"interested in **learning**,"* ❌ *"interested in **to learn**."*
* **Use the object case after a preposition:** ✅ *"between you and **me**,"* ❌ *"between you and **I**."*
* **A preposition never takes a verb as its object** (except in the gerund form).
* **Avoid unnecessary prepositions:** ❌ *"discuss **about** the plan"* → ✅ *"discuss the plan."*
